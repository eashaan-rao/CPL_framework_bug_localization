%% ABSTRACT
%% ===================================================================
\begin{abstract}
Many software projects, newly open-sourced codebases, systems migrating from
proprietary issue trackers, and projects with low bug-filing rates, begin active
development with only dozens to a few hundred verified bug-report-to-file mappings.
Modern deep learning bug localization models require substantially more paired
training data than this; a project with 110 verified reports, for example, yields
only approximately 22 labeled examples under a standard limited-data training
split, too few for reliable generalisation across a diverse codebase.
Within-project training is therefore not a viable path to effective bug localization
for these projects at the time they most need it.

Cross-project learning (CPL) directly targets this situation: train a model on a
data-rich source project, then apply or fine-tune it on the data-scarce target.
% Several CPL models have demonstrated the feasibility of knowledge transfer across
% projects~\citep{huo2019deep,zhu2020cooba,zhu2022bl,chakraborty2025blaze}. 
Yet every published CPL evaluation measures benefit against within-project training
\emph{with ample data}, the performance ceiling that data-scarce practitioners
cannot reach. Whether CPL actually helps the teams it is designed to serve, those
restricted to limited within-project training, has never been empirically tested.
Whether CPL findings hold across different model architectures is also unknown.

We fill both gaps. We evaluate three state-of-the-art bug localization models,
spanning embedding-based dual-encoding (BLAZE), convolutional reranking (TRANP-CNN),
and GNN-based adversarial adaptation (COOBA), across 63 source$\to$target project
pairs from 13 open-source Python projects under four experimental scenarios that
ablate target-project supervision from zero to 80\% of available labels, with
within-project \emph{limited} training as the primary baseline.

The crucial finding is that cross-project transfer with minimal target labels
significantly outperforms within-project limited training for \textbf{2 of 3}
architectural paradigms tested (both statistical significance and medium-to-large effect, $d \geq 0.5$):
embedding-based dual-encoders achieve a large effect
($d = 1.19$, win rate 90.5\%, $p < 0.001$) and CNN-based rerankers achieve a medium
effect ($d = 0.59$, win rate 68.3\%, $p < 0.001$). Strikingly, CNN-based rerankers
with 20\% target fine-tuning match full within-project training ($p = 0.19$, n.s.),
the only condition in this study where cross-project transfer closes the ample-data
gap entirely, and where it \emph{surpasses} within-project training for small-codebase
targets. GNN-based adversarial models show marginal gains only ($d = 0.27$, 31.7\%
negative transfer, near-zero cold-start). Additionally, embedding-based models enable
zero-label deployment: a source-trained model applied without any target annotations
matches within-project limited training in 74.6\% of pairs ($p = 0.415$, two-sided).
Cross-model direction agreement ranges from 41\% to 67\%, confirming that CPL outcomes
are architecture-dependent and the same project pair can benefit one model and harm
another.

For data-scarce projects, CPL is a viable and empirically grounded strategy in 2 of
3 tested architectural paradigms. Embedding-based tools can be deployed immediately
with no target annotations; CNN-based rerankers with 20\% target labels match the
ample-data training ceiling. The benefit is architecture-mediated rather than
universal: the model's inference-time adaptation mechanism determines CPL viability
more than the source$\to$target project pair does.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Bug localization, automatically identifying the source files responsible for a
reported defect, is a critical and labour-intensive task in software maintenance.
Deep learning-based bug localization models have demonstrated strong performance on
established benchmarks~\citep{lam2017bug,liang2022modeling,ciborowska2022fast}, but
their effectiveness rests on a prerequisite that is easy to overlook: they require
substantial labeled training data, namely paired examples of bug reports and the
ground-truth source files modified to resolve each defect. A project accumulates this
labeled corpus only through sustained active development, typically measured in
years of tracked issue resolution.

For many projects, this accumulation either never reaches a sufficient threshold or
starts from zero. Newly open-sourced codebases, projects migrating from proprietary
issue trackers, and systems with low bug-filing rates may have only dozens to a few
hundred verified mappings when a bug localization tool is most needed. To illustrate
the scale of this problem concretely: among the 13 open-source Python projects in
this study, the smallest has only 110 verified bug-report-to-file mappings,
yielding approximately 22 labeled training examples under a standard limited-data
split, too few for reliable generalisation across a diverse codebase. This is not a pathological edge case; it is representative of any project
in its first years of active bug tracking, and it is precisely the regime where
effective automated bug localization would be most valuable.

Cross-project learning (CPL) is a natural response to this constraint: train a bug
localization model on bug reports and source code from a data-rich source project,
then apply or fine-tune it on the data-scarce target. The underlying intuition is
that the relationship between natural-language defect descriptions and their
corresponding code locations may transfer across projects that share a programming
language, coding style, or functional domain. Several CPL models have been proposed
and shown to be feasible: TRANP-CNN~\citep{huo2019deep} learns shared bug-report--code
representations across projects; COOBA~\citep{zhu2020cooba} uses adversarial domain
adaptation to produce project-invariant representations; BL-GAN~\citep{zhu2022bl}
augments scarce target labels with generative models; and BLAZE~\citep{chakraborty2025blaze}
extends contrastive cross-project transfer to multiple programming languages.

Despite this body of work, a fundamental question remains unanswered: does CPL
actually help the practitioners it is designed to serve? Each of the above studies
demonstrates that its CPL model outperforms an ablated baseline or an earlier CPL
method, but in each case the within-project comparator draws on the majority of the
target project's available labeled history, a data volume that practitioners in
the data-scarce regime cannot match. The experiments establish that CPL beats a
standard they cannot reach; whether CPL beats the limited within-project training
they \emph{can} actually perform is never the comparison made. In the analogous task
of cross-project defect prediction, a systematic survey of 90 studies found that
cross-project transfer is context-dependent, helping in some conditions and
hurting in others, with no single factor reliably predicting success~\citep{hosseini2017systematic}.
The question ``does CPL help teams that already have limited within-project data?''
is not answered anywhere in the existing bug localization literature.

A second gap amplifies the uncertainty. Every published CPL paper evaluates a single
model of its own design. Whether the demonstrated CPL benefit is a property of the
cross-project paradigm, or an artefact of a particular model's inductive biases,
is therefore unknown. A practitioner who reads one CPL paper and selects a different
bug localization tool cannot know whether the findings apply to their choice. No
existing study uses multiple CPL models as independent instruments across the same
project pairs to test whether CPL outcomes are consistent across architectures.

We fill both gaps with a controlled empirical study of CPL viability. We evaluate
three state-of-the-art bug localization models spanning architecturally distinct
paradigms, embedding-based dual-encoding (BLAZE), graph-neural-network structural
encoding (COOBA), and convolutional text-code alignment (TRANP-CNN), across
63 directional source$\to$target project pairs drawn from 13 open-source Python
projects spanning four software domains (Data Science/ML, Developer Tools,
Systems/Cloud, and Applications). For each pair we measure performance under four
experimental scenarios designed to isolate the contribution of cross-project
knowledge while controlling for the amount of target-project supervision. The
primary baseline throughout is \emph{within-project limited training}, the data
a newly active project actually has, not the ample-data ceiling that prior work
has used.

A core methodological contribution, independent of the empirical findings, is the
evaluation protocol itself. To our knowledge, no existing CPL study evaluates more than one model:
each prior paper proposes its own architecture, selects its own project corpus, and
defines its own within-project comparator independently. Cross-paper comparisons
therefore conflate architectural differences with differences in experimental
conditions, making it impossible to attribute observed CPL outcomes to any specific
cause. In this study, all three models are evaluated on the same 63 project pairs,
the same bug reports, the same train/test splits, the same FAISS retrieval indexes,
and the same primary baseline, three architecturally distinct models as
\textbf{independent scientific instruments under an identical experimental protocol}.
This uniform design is what allows conclusions
about \emph{why} CPL succeeds or fails to be drawn with confidence.

Our results answer the practitioner's question with empirical evidence across three
independent architectural instruments. Cross-project transfer with minimal target
labels significantly outperforms within-project limited training for \textbf{2 of 3}
benchmarked architectural paradigms: embedding-based dual-encoders show a large effect
(win rate 90.5\%, Cohen's $d = 1.19$, $p < 0.001$, gain-to-loss asymmetry 27:1),
and CNN-based rerankers show a medium effect (win rate 68.3\%, $d = 0.59$,
$p < 0.001$, asymmetry 2.8:1). A particularly striking finding is that CNN-based
rerankers with 20\% target fine-tuning are statistically indistinguishable from
full within-project training ($p = 0.19$, n.s.), the only condition in this
study where cross-project transfer closes the ample-data gap, and where it exceeds
the within-project ceiling for small-codebase targets.
Beyond the transfer-with-labels result, a source-trained embedding model applied
\emph{with zero target annotations} matches within-project limited training in
74.6\% of pairs ($p = 0.415$, two-sided), eliminating the annotation requirement
entirely. GNN-based adversarial models show smaller marginal gains ($d = 0.27$)
with a steeper negative transfer risk (31.7\% of pairs). The three-model negative
transfer spectrum, 3.2\% (BLAZE), 27.0\% (TRANP-CNN), 31.7\% (COOBA),
provides a practical risk ranking for CPL adoption. Cross-model direction agreement
ranges from 41\% to 67\%, and source within-project quality does not
reliably predict transfer success for any model. This architecture-dependence is
not merely a statistical finding: a practitioner deploying CPL must validate the
benefit empirically for their chosen model, as the same project pair can yield
large gains for one architecture and active harm for another.

Answering this question rigorously is non-trivial, presenting three distinct
challenges. \textbf{(C1)} Attributing CPL outcomes to model architecture rather than
experimental conditions requires eliminating heterogeneity in training data, evaluation
corpora, and baselines across architectures, a standard that no prior CPL study
has enforced. \textbf{(C2)} Choosing an appropriate primary baseline is conceptually
subtle: practitioners in the data-scarce regime cannot reach the ample-data ceiling
used in prior work, so any comparison against that ceiling answers the wrong question.
\textbf{(C3)} Assembling a project corpus large enough for statistical power across
diverse source$\to$target pairs without exceeding a fixed compute budget requires a
principled structured pair selection strategy rather than a na\"{i}ve all-pairs design.

The contributions of this work directly address these challenges:
\begin{itemize}
  \item A \textbf{controlled multi-instrument evaluation framework} in which three
        architecturally distinct bug localization models are assessed under an
        identical experimental protocol, the same project pairs, bug reports,
        data splits, FAISS retrieval indexes (shared across all three models from
        identical BGE embeddings), and primary baseline. To our knowledge,
        no prior CPL study provides this; the framework is itself a methodological
        contribution that enables attribution of CPL outcomes to model architecture
        rather than experimental heterogeneity (addressing C1).
  \item The first systematic empirical comparison of CPL against the
        practically relevant baseline of within-project limited training (addressing
        C2), across 63 source$\to$target pairs drawn from 13 projects via a
        principled structured selection that maximises analytical coverage within
        a fixed compute budget (addressing C3). We demonstrate that CPL significantly
        outperforms this baseline for 2 of 3 architectural paradigms, with
        large-to-medium effect sizes.
  \item Evidence that (a) zero-label cross-project deployment is viable for
        embedding-based bug localization, matching within-project limited training
        without any target annotations in 74.6\% of pairs; and (b) CNN-based
        rerankers with 20\% target fine-tuning achieve performance statistically
        indistinguishable from full within-project training, the highest CPL
        ceiling observed in this study.
  \item Characterisation of the conditions, target codebase size, domain
        alignment, and model architecture, under which CPL is most and least
        effective, including a three-tier negative transfer risk profile
        (3.2\%$\to$27.0\%$\to$31.7\% across the three architectural paradigms).
  \item A cross-model consistency analysis establishing that CPL benefit is
        architecture-dependent, not a universal property of the paradigm: pairwise
        agreement ranges from 41\% to 67\%, and source within-project quality
        does not reliably predict transfer success for any model.
\end{itemize}

The rest of the paper is organised as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews
related work on bug localization and CPL. Section~\ref{sec:design} describes the
study design. Section~\ref{sec:results} presents results for each research question.
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses practical implications and threats to validity.
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Bug Localization}

Bug localization is the task of ranking source files (or functions) by their
likelihood of containing the defect described in a natural-language bug report.
Early approaches relied on information-retrieval similarity between bug report text
and source file content \citep{saha2013improving,ye2014learning}, using measures
such as BM25 or VSM over code tokens. These methods are simple and require no
training data, but their ranking quality is limited by vocabulary mismatch between
bug report prose and code identifiers.

Deep learning approaches substantially improved on IR baselines by learning
joint representations of bug reports and code. \citet{lam2017bug} (DNNLOC)
introduced a multi-layer neural network that combines textual similarity, file
change history, and structural features, achieving significant improvements over
IR on Java benchmarks. \citet{liang2022modeling} extended this to function-level
localisation using a graph neural network that captures call dependencies.
\citet{ciborowska2022fast} replaced conventional encoders with pre-trained BERT
representations over code changesets, demonstrating that transfer from large
pre-trained models reduces the labeled-data requirement. A recent survey by
\citet{niu2025deep} covers the full landscape of deep learning approaches and
confirms that labeled within-project data remains the standard assumption across
this literature. The benchmark of \citet{lee2018bench4bl} provides standardised
evaluation corpora covering 51 Java projects, and has become a reference point
for comparing bug localization methods.

A consistent limitation of all within-project approaches is that they assume
the target project has accumulated sufficient labeled bug reports for training.
This assumption fails for new or data-scarce projects, which is the exact
regime that motivates the present study.

\subsection{Cross-Project Bug Localization}

Cross-project bug localization (CPL) addresses data scarcity by transferring
knowledge from a source project to a target. \citet{huo2019deep} proposed
TRANP-CNN, the first deep learning CPL model: a convolutional network that
learns shared representations of bug-report--code pairs from a source project
and applies them to a target, but evaluates only against an ample-data
within-project baseline, leaving the practically relevant data-scarce comparison
unanswered. \citet{zhu2020cooba} introduced COOBA, which uses adversarial domain
adaptation \citep{ganin2016domain} via a gradient reversal layer to learn
domain-invariant representations from AST-structured code embeddings, but, as
we demonstrate empirically, the adversarial objective requires target-side signal
at training time and collapses to near-zero performance without it.
\citet{zhu2022bl} proposed BL-GAN, a semi-supervised approach using generative
adversarial networks to augment scarce target labels, but does not test whether
CPL helps when the practitioner has only limited within-project data as the
available alternative. \citet{chakraborty2025blaze} proposed BLAZE, a
dual-encoder contrastive model using dynamic chunking and hard-negative mining
that evaluates cross-project and cross-language transfer, but evaluates a
single architecture, leaving cross-model CPL consistency unknown.

Despite this body of work, a critical gap remains: each paper proposes and
evaluates its own model, typically comparing against within-project ample-data
baselines or earlier CPL methods. To our knowledge, \emph{no existing study}
(i) uses multiple CPL models as independent instruments, (ii) evaluates against
the practitioner-relevant limited within-project baseline, (iii) characterises
the conditions governing CPL success at scale, or (iv) tests cross-model
consistency of CPL findings.

A distinct emerging paradigm employs large language models in agentic interactive
workflows, such as SWE-agent and Agentless, that bypass labeled-data
requirements by leveraging the model's pre-training directly for fault localisation
and patch generation. While these agent-based approaches are promising for projects
willing to accept cloud-inference costs and privacy implications, the supervised CPL
setting studied here remains directly relevant for offline-deployable, cost-sensitive,
or privacy-constrained projects, and for settings where fine-grained ranking across a
large codebase is required rather than targeted patch generation. The conditions
governing CPL success identified in this study, domain alignment, embedding
quality, and inference-time adaptation mechanisms, may also inform future work on
how agents select which code to prioritise during fault localisation.

\subsection{Transfer Learning in Software Engineering}

The broader literature on cross-project transfer in software engineering provides
useful context. Cross-project defect prediction, predicting whether a software
module will be defective, using training data from another project, has been
studied systematically over many years. \citet{hosseini2017systematic} survey
90 such studies and conclude that cross-project transfer is context-dependent:
it helps in some conditions and hurts in others, with no single factor reliably
predicting transfer success across all settings. This finding from the defect
prediction literature directly motivates our characterisation of the conditions
governing CPL success in bug localisation, and our focus on negative transfer
as a deployment risk. The methodological parallel is informative: like defect
prediction, bug localisation is a within-project learning task where CPL is
possible but not guaranteed to be beneficial.

The theoretical basis for adversarial domain adaptation used in COOBA is
domain-adversarial neural networks \citep{ganin2016domain}, which minimise a
feature-level divergence between source and target distributions by training a
domain discriminator whose gradients are reversed during feature extraction.
This technique has been applied in a number of software engineering transfer
learning settings, but its effectiveness for cross-project code understanding
depends critically on whether the feature extractor captures representations that
are genuinely domain-invariant, an assumption we test empirically.

\subsection{Pre-trained Code Embeddings: Background and Design Motivation}

Recent advances in pre-trained code embeddings have substantially raised the quality
of representations available to both within-project and cross-project models. This
subsection describes the embedding backbone used in our study and motivates why it
serves as a controlled baseline shared across all three architectures.

The \texttt{BAAI/bge-code-v1} model \citep{bge_code}, a 1536-dimensional
Qwen2-based decoder pre-trained on a diverse multilingual code corpus, provides
the embedding backbone for all three models in this study, ensuring that representation
quality is not a confound when comparing architectures, and focusing attention on
the reranking and adaptation stages where model-architectural differences are
consequential.

Together, these four subsections of the literature frame the design choices in
Section~\ref{sec:design}: the shared BGE backbone controls for representation quality
(eliminating it as a confound), the three architecturally distinct models serve as
independent instruments for testing CPL consistency, and the limited within-project
baseline directly addresses the evaluation gap identified in the CPL literature.

\section{Study Design}
\label{sec:design}

The study is organised around a single methodological principle: to make CPL
benefit attributable to the cross-project paradigm and to model architecture,
rather than to incidental differences in training data, input representations,
or evaluation conditions. Every design choice documented in this section,
the shared embedding backbone, the identical datasets and held-out test splits,
the four-scenario training framework, the choice of primary baseline, and the
structured pair selection, follows from this principle and is justified
explicitly below.

A critical element of this controlled design is the shared retrieval infrastructure.
A \textbf{FAISS IndexFlatIP} retrieval stage, built from identical BGE embeddings
across all target projects, serves as the first-stage candidate pool shared across
all three models.

To our knowledge, no existing CPL study in bug localization evaluates more than one
model against a common experimental protocol. Each prior paper proposes its own model,
selects its own projects and bug corpora, and defines its own within-project comparator.
Under this fragmented evaluation practice, cross-paper comparisons conflate
differences in model architecture with differences in experimental conditions,
making it impossible to attribute observed CPL outcomes to any specific cause.
We depart from this practice by treating three architecturally distinct models
as \textbf{independent scientific instruments}, all evaluated under an identical
experimental protocol: the same projects, the same bug reports, the same
train/test splits, the same FAISS retrieval indexes, the same evaluation metrics,
and the same primary baseline. This controlled, multi-instrument design is what
allows conclusions about \emph{why} CPL succeeds or fails, not just \emph{that}
it does, and it is, to our knowledge, the first such design in the CPL bug
localization literature.

\subsection{Research Questions}

This study is organised around three research questions, each targeting a distinct
aspect of CPL viability.

\begin{description}
  \item[\textbf{RQ1}] \emph{Does cross-project learning outperform within-project
    training under label scarcity?} This tests the core CPL premise against the
    practitioner-relevant baseline established in Section~\ref{sec:intro}: a project
    restricted to the limited within-project data it actually has, not the ample-data
    ceiling prior CPL work has used.

  \item[\textbf{RQ2}] \emph{What project-level characteristics determine whether CPL
    succeeds or fails?} Even if CPL is beneficial on average, practitioners need to
    know \emph{when} it is and is not appropriate. This question identifies moderating
    conditions (target size, domain alignment) and quantifies the risk of negative
    transfer.

  \item[\textbf{RQ3}] \emph{How consistent are CPL outcomes across different model
    implementations?} If CPL benefit is a property of the paradigm, outcomes should
    be consistent across architectures. If it is model-specific, practitioners
    cannot generalise findings from one tool to another. This question also
    evaluates whether project metadata reliably guides source project selection.
\end{description}

\subsection{Benchmarked Models}
\label{sec:models}

A central design choice is to evaluate CPL using three architecturally distinct
bug localization models rather than a single representative. This guards against
conclusions that are artefacts of one model's inductive bias. The three models
span the dominant paradigms in the current literature.

\paragraph{BLAZE \citep{chakraborty2025blaze}.}
BLAZE is a dual-encoder contrastive model that retrieves the top-300 candidate
files via the shared FAISS index and re-ranks them by maximum cosine similarity.
Its backbone is
\texttt{BAAI/bge-code-v1} \citep{bge_code}, frozen during training. Two modal-specific
residual adapter modules, one for bug reports (prefixed \texttt{search\_query:}) and
one for code chunks (prefixed \texttt{search\_document:}), are trained with
in-batch NTXentLoss ($\tau = 0.3$) using hard-negative and hard-positive scaling.
At evaluation time, each source file is scored as the maximum cosine similarity
over its 512-token chunks (MaxSim aggregation). This architecture is sensitive to
the most relevant passage within a long file, which is advantageous for large
codebases.

\paragraph{COOBA \citep{zhu2020cooba}.}
COOBA is a graph neural network model that encodes source files as Abstract Syntax
Tree (AST) graphs processed by graph convolutional layers (GCNConv) with global
mean pooling. Bug reports are encoded by a bidirectional LSTM over GloVe-300 token
embeddings. A shared BGE-based extractor produces domain-invariant representations
via a Gradient Reversal Layer \citep{ganin2016domain} placed before a binary project
discriminator: the shared extractor is simultaneously optimised to minimise ranking
loss and to prevent the discriminator from distinguishing source from target project
representations. This adversarial objective ($\lambda_\mathrm{adv} = 0.1$) is the
core CPL mechanism. COOBA uses FAISS top-300 retrieval as a first stage.

\paragraph{TRANP-CNN \citep{huo2019deep}.}
TRANP-CNN is a CNN-based reranker that processes (bug report, candidate file) pairs
through parallel convolutional feature extractors (kernel sizes 3, 4, 5) and produces
scalar relevance scores. It is trained with a margin ranking loss on FAISS top-300
candidates. TRANP-CNN represents the earliest deep learning CPL approach in our
benchmark and provides a third architectural perspective, convolutional text-code
alignment, complementing the embedding-based and graph-structural paradigms of
BLAZE and COOBA.

Table~\ref{tab:models} summarises the three models' key properties. Together they
span the dominant implementation paradigms in the current CPL literature:
embedding-based dual-encoding (BLAZE), graph-neural-network structural encoding
(COOBA), and convolutional text-code alignment (TRANP-CNN), providing a diverse
basis for testing whether CPL viability is a property of the paradigm or of
specific architectural choices.

\subsection{Dataset Selection and Curation}
\label{sec:dataset}

\paragraph{Source Benchmarks.}
The candidate pool was assembled by consolidating projects from five established
bug localization benchmarks: \textbf{Bench4BL} \citep{lee2018bench4bl},
\textbf{BeetleBox} \citep{chakraborty2025blaze}, \textbf{LCA} \citep{bogomolov2024long},
\textbf{SWE-bench} \citep{jimenez2023swe}, and the dataset of \citet{ye2014learning}.
Drawing from multiple sources avoids the selection bias of any single benchmark and
maximises diversity of project types and domains. The initial pool comprised over 500
unique repositories across multiple languages.

\paragraph{Filtering Criteria.}
Projects were filtered by language-stratified thresholds ensuring statistically
adequate bug report volumes. Python and Java projects required at least 100 unique
bug reports with verified ground-truth file mappings (commit SHAs); under-represented
languages required at least 20. Reports with missing annotations, ambiguous multi-file
patches ($> 10$ files), or non-source patches (documentation, CI configuration) were
discarded. This filtering step reduced the pool from over 500 repositories to
\textbf{98 curated repositories} (41 Python, 34 Java, 9 Kotlin, 6 JavaScript, 5
C++, 3 Go). Although the pool spans six languages, Phase 1 restricts to Python to
establish a language-homogeneous baseline; cross-language extension is deferred to
future work (Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}).

\paragraph{Phase 1 Scope: 13 Python Projects.}
Of the 41 qualifying Python repositories, \textbf{13 projects} simultaneously
satisfy three diversity criteria that together ensure the corpus spans meaningful
variation in the factors most likely to affect CPL outcomes:
(i) \emph{codebase size}: 35K--572K lines of code (spanning one order of magnitude);
(ii) \emph{domain}: four distinct application domains (Data Science/ML, Developer Tools,
Systems/Cloud, Applications); (iii) \emph{bug corpus size}: 110--1,336 bug reports.
Critically, these 13 projects constitute the \textbf{complete} qualifying set from
the 41-project Python pool: every project satisfying all three criteria simultaneously
is included, and none were excluded after the filtering stage.
Table~\ref{tab:dataset} summarises the 13 projects.

The Python-only scope is also motivated by the computational scale of the study.
Across 63 source$\to$target pairs, four experimental scenarios, and three models,
the complete evaluation amounts to \textbf{756 independent experiment runs}
(63 pairs $\times$ 4 scenarios $\times$ 3 models), each involving model training
from scratch, FAISS index construction over the target codebase, and per-bug
evaluation over held-out test sets. Completing this evaluation required approximately

\subsection{Data Preprocessing}
\label{sec:preprocessing}

The preprocessing pipeline runs once per project and persists outputs as
project-level databases, avoiding redundant recomputation across the 756 experiment
runs.

\paragraph{Bug Report Curation.}
Raw bug reports are extracted from the five benchmarks and cross-referenced against
repository commit history to obtain ground-truth file mappings. Each report is
uniquely identified by its commit SHA. Reports with missing SHAs, ambiguous patches
($>10$ files), or non-source patches are discarded. The resulting bug metadata
database contains \texttt{\{bug\_id, bug\_text, gt\_file\_path, commit\_sha\}}.

\paragraph{Source Code Embedding.}
All source files are extracted at the commit corresponding to each bug report
(project state at filing time). Files are encoded with \textbf{BAAI/bge-code-v1}
\citep{bge_code}, a 1536-dimensional Qwen2-based decoder pre-trained for code
understanding. Files exceeding 512 tokens are split into overlapping chunks
(\textbf{512 tokens, 50-token overlap}); chunk embeddings are averaged to produce
a single file-level \texttt{float32} vector. Bug report texts are encoded analogously.
The pipeline uses producer-consumer multiprocessing (8 CPU workers) with batched
GPU inference (batch size 256), processing all 13 projects in approximately
6 hours on a single A100 GPU.

Per-project outputs are two binary databases: a \emph{blob embedding database}
mapping each source file's blob SHA to its 1536-dimensional embedding, and a
\emph{bug metadata database} mapping each bug ID to its embedding and ground-truth
annotation. All three models use these same databases, ensuring that any observed
performance differences are attributable to model architecture rather than input
representation quality.

\subsection{Evaluation Pipeline}
\label{sec:pipeline}

\paragraph{Retriever-Reranker Pipeline.}
All three models use the same two-stage pipeline. In the retrieval stage, a
\textbf{FAISS IndexFlatIP} index is built from L2-normalised BGE file embeddings
(inner product = cosine similarity). Each bug report is encoded, normalised, and
queried to retrieve the top-$K = 300$ candidates. Retrieval recall at $K = 300$
varies across projects (further discussed in Section~\ref{sec:threats}); ground-truth
files not retrieved at this stage are missed at the reranking step. In the reranking
stage, each model processes the candidate set and re-sorts by relevance score.
TRANP-CNN and COOBA process (bug report, candidate file) pairs and output relevance
scores. BLAZE re-ranks the top-300 candidates by maximum cosine similarity between
the fine-tuned bug report embedding and each file's chunk embeddings (MaxSim
aggregation). Evaluation metrics are computed over the re-sorted list.

\subsection{Training Scenarios}
\label{sec:scenarios}

The four training scenarios are the core methodological contribution of the study
design. They address a specific gap in how CPL has been evaluated in the existing
literature. Every prior CPL bug localization paper evaluates its model against a
within-project baseline trained on the \emph{majority} of the target project's
available labels, the performance ceiling achievable with ample supervision.
This comparison answers the wrong question for data-scarce practitioners: they
cannot reach that ceiling. Demonstrating that a CPL model beats a standard that
no data-scarce team can achieve tells those teams nothing useful about whether CPL
will help them.

The four scenarios are designed to answer the practitioner's actual question by
forming a clean \textbf{ablation over the supervision signal available at training
time}, from none at all, to limited target data only, to limited target data
augmented with full source data, to the ample-data ceiling. The test set is held
constant throughout (20\% of target bug reports, random seed 42, identical across
all three models); only the training data composition changes. This structure
enables pairwise comparisons that isolate the marginal value of each source of
supervision precisely.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{WP-small} (Within-Project, Limited): Model trained on approximately
    20\% of the target bug corpus, the data a newly active project actually has.
    This scenario serves as the \emph{primary baseline} for all CPL comparisons
    throughout the study. Choosing WP-small rather than WP-large as the primary
    baseline is a deliberate departure from prior CPL work. CPL is designed to help
    data-scarce projects; measuring its benefit relative to a data-scarce baseline
    directly tests whether it delivers on that design intent.

  \item \textbf{WP-large} (Within-Project, Ample): Model trained on 80\% of the
    target bug corpus. Included as the performance ceiling achievable with maximum
    within-project supervision, not as a CPL comparator, but as a reference
    showing how far CPL closes the gap toward the ideal case. The comparison
    WP-large $-$ CP-transfer quantifies what CPL still cannot achieve; the comparison
    CP-transfer $-$ WP-small quantifies what it gains over the practical baseline.

  \item \textbf{CP-cold-start} (Cross-Project, Zero-Shot): Model trained on 100\%
    of the source project's bug reports, with \emph{zero} target annotations.
    Tests zero-label deployment: can a source-trained model be applied directly to
    a new target with no annotation effort? This represents the minimum viable
    deployment scenario for a project at day zero of bug tracking. If cold-start
    performance matches WP-small, annotation cost can be eliminated entirely.

  \item \textbf{CP-transfer} (Cross-Project, Fine-Tuned): Model trained on 100\%
    of source bugs plus approximately 20\% of target bugs. The target-data budget
    is deliberately matched to WP-small. This is a critical design choice: because
    both CP-transfer and WP-small use the same number of target-project labels,
    any performance advantage of CP-transfer over WP-small is attributable to the
    cross-project knowledge from the source project, not to additional target
    data. Without this budget-matching, the comparison would be confounded by
    differential access to target-project supervision.
\end{itemize}

This scenario framework is applied identically to all three benchmarked models.
No model-specific adjustments to scenario definitions, data splits, or evaluation
protocols are made. This uniform treatment is what makes the cross-model comparison
in RQ3 valid: observed differences in CPL effectiveness arise from the models
themselves, not from heterogeneous experimental conditions.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}

Five complementary metrics provide a complete picture of retrieval breadth and
ranking precision, computed over the re-ranked candidate list for each test bug
report and averaged over all test reports.

\textbf{Top-$K$ Recall} ($K \in \{1, 5, 10\}$): fraction of bug reports for which
the ground-truth file appears within the top $K$ positions. Top-1 captures
precision-at-one; Top-10 captures shortlist breadth.

\textbf{Mean Average Precision (MAP)}: in the single-ground-truth setting used here,
MAP reduces to $1/r_i$ averaged over all bug reports $i$, where $r_i$ is the rank
of the correct file.

\textbf{Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR)}: mean of $1/\text{rank}$ across all bug reports.
Identical to MAP in the single-relevant-document case; both are reported for
comparability with prior literature.

MRR and MAP diverge from Top-$K$ when the correct file is retrieved but ranked
outside the top-$K$ shortlist. This divergence is itself informative: it reveals
whether failures are catastrophic (file not retrieved) or marginal (file ranked
too low).

\subsection{Domain Gap Measurement}
\label{sec:domaingap}

To quantify structural dissimilarity between source and target projects, a
\textbf{domain gap score} is computed for every pair by training a binary logistic
regression classifier to distinguish the two projects' file embeddings. Procedure:
(i) all file-level BGE embeddings from the source project are assigned label 0 and
from the target project label 1; (ii) a balanced 80/20 stratified split is
constructed; (iii) a \texttt{LogisticRegression} classifier with $\ell_2$
regularisation (default $C = 1.0$, up to 1000 iterations) is trained and evaluated
on the held-out split; (iv) the test accuracy is recorded as the domain gap score.

A score of 0.5 indicates that the two projects are indistinguishable in embedding
space (no domain shift); a score of 1.0 indicates fully disjoint embedding regions
(maximum domain shift). The linear probe approach has been widely used as a
separability diagnostic in the domain adaptation literature \citep{ganin2016domain}
and avoids the risk of a more powerful classifier overfitting on small embedding
datasets. Domain gap scores for all pairs are computed once and shared across all
three model evaluations.

\subsection{Statistical Analysis Methods}
\label{sec:statmethods}

\paragraph{RQ1, Wilcoxon Signed-Rank Test.}
To assess whether CP-transfer outperforms WP-small, a \textbf{Wilcoxon signed-rank
test} \citep{wilcoxon1945} is applied to each metric separately. This non-parametric
paired test is appropriate because: observations are naturally paired (each
source$\to$target pair contributes one CPT and one WPS result under identical test
conditions); and the distribution of improvements cannot be assumed normal across
such a diverse corpus. A one-sided alternative is tested ($H_1$: CPT $>$ WPS).
Effect sizes are reported as Cohen's $d$ \citep{cohen1988statistical}:
$d \geq 0.8$ large, $\geq 0.5$ medium, $\geq 0.2$ small.

Significance thresholds: $\star\star\star$ ($p < 0.001$), $\star\star$ ($p < 0.01$),
$\star$ ($p < 0.05$), n.s. ($p \geq 0.05$). Bonferroni correction is applied where
multiple comparisons are made within a single test family.

\paragraph{RQ2, Spearman Rank Correlation.}
To identify project-level features predictive of CPL gain, \textbf{Spearman's $\rho$}
is computed between each feature and CPT$-$WPS gain across all pairs. Spearman's
$\rho$ is preferred over Pearson's $r$ because the corpus spans several orders of
magnitude in codebase size and bug count, making linear association inappropriate.

\paragraph{RQ3, Source Selection Evaluation.}
To evaluate source selection heuristics, \textbf{Kendall's $\tau$}
\citep{kendall1938new} is computed between the ordering produced by each heuristic
and the oracle ordering (sorted by actual CP-transfer MRR) for each target project
with at least three candidate sources. \textbf{Hit@1} (fraction of targets for which
the heuristic selects the oracle-best source) and the mean percentage of oracle
performance achieved by the selected source are also reported.

\paragraph{Statistical Power and Sample Size.}
With $n = 63$ paired observations, a one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test achieves
$>$99\% power to detect a medium effect ($d = 0.5$) at $\alpha = 0.05$, and
$>$80\% power to detect a small effect ($d = 0.2$). The study corpus is therefore
adequately powered for all comparisons reported in RQ1. Power was estimated
via simulation under a normal approximation to the signed-rank statistic
($10^{4}$ replications, two-parameter effect-size grid).

\subsubsection{Statistical Significance and Effect Size}

Table~\ref{tab:wilcoxon} presents Wilcoxon signed-rank test results and Cohen's $d$
for the CP-transfer versus WP-small comparison across all metrics and models.

For embedding-based models (BLAZE), cross-project transfer significantly outperforms
within-project limited training across all five metrics ($p < 0.001$, $\star\star\star$
on all metrics). Win rates range from 82.5\% (Top-1) to 93.7\% (MAP). Effect sizes
are large by Cohen's convention on all metrics ($d = 1.04$--$1.19$), indicating that
the CPL advantage is not only statistically reliable but practically substantial.
A large effect ($d > 1$) means the CPL gain is detectable even in studies with as
few as 10 project pairs — the benefit is unmistakable in scale.

For CNN-based rerankers (TRANP-CNN), cross-project transfer also significantly
outperforms within-project limited training across all five metrics. Win rates range
from 54.8\% (Top-1) to 71.4\% (MAP). Effect sizes are medium ($d = 0.32$--$0.59$),
with $p < 0.001$ on MRR, MAP, and Top-5; $p < 0.01$ on Top-1; and $p < 0.05$ on
Top-10. These results establish TRANP-CNN as the second model, alongside BLAZE,
for which CPL is statistically significant and practically meaningful across the full
metric set. A medium effect means the advantage is real and reproducible, though
a larger sample would be needed to detect it reliably in a smaller study.

For GNN-based adversarial models (COOBA), the CPL advantage is present but marginal:
significant on MRR, MAP, Top-1, and Top-10 ($p = 0.010$--$0.043$, $\star$) and
borderline on Top-5 ($p = 0.058$). Win rates range from 43.5\% (Top-1) to
60.3\% (MRR/MAP), close to chance on several metrics. Effect sizes are small
($d = 0.20$--$0.31$) — statistically detectable at 63 pairs but too small to
provide consistent benefit across individual deployments.

We classify an architectural paradigm as exhibiting \emph{practically beneficial}
CPL if it meets both criteria: statistical significance ($p < 0.05$, Bonferroni-adjusted)
\emph{and} at least a medium effect size ($d \geq 0.5$). The medium-effect threshold
is the conventional minimum for consistent individual-deployment benefit; a small
effect is statistically detectable at $n = 63$ but too small to reliably manifest
in any single project pair. Under this criterion, BLAZE ($d = 1.19$) and TRANP-CNN
($d = 0.59$) meet both criteria; COOBA ($d = 0.27$, $p < 0.05$ on most metrics)
is statistically detectable but falls below the practical-benefit threshold.

Taken together, \textbf{2 of 3 benchmarked architectural paradigms show statistically
significant CPL benefit with a medium-to-large effect size.} This cross-architecture
replication is the primary evidence that CPL is a viable strategy for data-scarce
projects, rather than an artefact of any particular model's inductive bias.
The three-tier effect size profile ($d \approx 1.19$ for BLAZE, $0.59$ for
TRANP-CNN, $0.27$ for COOBA) maps directly to deployment risk: BLAZE and
TRANP-CNN are architectures where CPL can be recommended with confidence;
COOBA requires empirical validation before adoption.

\subsubsection{Win/Loss Asymmetry}

A critical practical question is not just whether CPL helps on average, but
\emph{what happens when it does not help}. Asymmetry between gains and losses is
practically more important than win rate alone: a model that wins 60\% of the time
but loses catastrophically in the other 40\% is worse than one that wins 80\% with
smaller, negligible losses. The CPL gain distribution for embedding-based models
(BLAZE) shows a pronounced asymmetry: when CP-transfer outperforms WP-small it
does so substantially (median win $= +0.134$ MRR), but when it does not, the loss
is negligible (median loss $= -0.005$ MRR). The asymmetry ratio, median gain
divided by median loss magnitude, is $27\times$ for MRR: BLAZE's expected benefit
of adoption is 27 times larger than the expected cost of failure, making it a
low-risk default for CPL deployment.

For CNN-based rerankers (TRANP-CNN), the asymmetry ratio is $2.8\times$ (median
win $= +0.068$ MRR vs.\ median loss $= -0.025$ MRR), a moderate asymmetry
that still favours adoption but with meaningfully higher loss exposure than
embedding-based models. Teams using TRANP-CNN should expect that roughly 1 in 3
deployments will underperform limited within-project training, though the gains
in the other deployments are substantially larger.

For GNN-based adversarial models (COOBA), the asymmetry ratio falls to only
$1.5\times$ (median win $= +0.032$ MRR vs.\ median loss $= -0.022$ MRR),
a near-symmetric risk profile where gains and losses are comparable in magnitude.
A team adopting COOBA for CPL should expect to lose as much as they gain in nearly
a third of deployments — a risk profile that calls for empirical validation before
committing to the strategy.
The three-way asymmetry spectrum ($27\times \to 2.8\times \to 1.5\times$) mirrors
the effect-size ordering and provides a practical risk ranking for CPL adoption
decisions. Figure \ref{fig:gain_distribution} illustrates the gain distributions.

\subsubsection{Zero-Label Deployment: The Cold-Start Finding}

Cold-start CPL — applying a source-trained model with zero target annotations —
represents the most practically valuable scenario for data-scarce teams: it removes
the annotation bottleneck entirely. Testing whether it is viable is therefore
central to the utility case for CPL.

For embedding-based models, CP-cold-start achieves MRR = 0.262 versus WP-small
MRR = 0.256, a difference that is not statistically significant (Wilcoxon,
$p = 0.415$, two-sided). A source-trained model applied to a new project
\emph{without any target annotations} is statistically equivalent to training on
20\% of the target project's own data. This finding holds across all five
evaluation metrics.

Figure \ref{fig:coldstart_heatmap} shows the cold-start viability heatmap across
all source$\to$target combinations. In 74.6\% of pairs (47/63), the cold-start
model achieves an MRR of at least 0.20 — a threshold corresponding to the median
FAISS-only retrieval performance, below which ranking quality is negligible for
practical developer triage — suggesting that annotation effort can be eliminated
entirely for the majority of deployment scenarios when an appropriate source project
is available. For a newly open-sourced project with no
labeled bugs at all, this means a BLAZE model trained on any within-domain source project
can be deployed immediately at near within-project performance — zero annotation cost.

GNN-based adversarial models (COOBA) collapse to near-zero performance in cold-start
(MRR = 0.027), statistically indistinguishable from the raw FAISS retrieval baseline
(MRR = 0.029) and far below WP-small (MRR = 0.138, $p < 0.001$). CNN-based rerankers
(TRANP-CNN) also fail cold-start, though to a lesser degree: MRR = 0.075 versus
WP-small MRR = 0.326 ($p < 0.001$). TRANP-CNN cold-start is 2.6$\times$ the FAISS
baseline, indicating some residual utility from source-trained convolution weights, but
still represents only 23\% of within-project limited training performance. Both scalar-ranking
architectures require target-side calibration: without target examples, their scoring
functions remain anchored to source-project distributions and do not transfer to the
target's ranking difficulty. The cold-start dichotomy is architectural: BLAZE's
embedding similarity generalises directly to unseen targets, while TRANP-CNN and
COOBA's learned ranking scores do not.

Cross-project transfer with minimal target labels significantly outperforms

paradigms.
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep,topsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Embedding-based (BLAZE):} large effect ($d = 1.19$, win rate 90.5\%,
        $p < 0.001$). Zero-label deployment matches within-project limited training,
        eliminating annotation cost in 74.6\% of pairs. 27:1 gain-to-loss asymmetry.
  \item \textbf{CNN-based reranker (TRANP-CNN):} medium effect ($d = 0.59$, win rate
        68.3\%, $p < 0.001$). CP-transfer is statistically indistinguishable from
        WP-large ($p = 0.19$), the only architecture matching the ample-data ceiling
        with just 20\% target labels. 2.8:1 gain-to-loss asymmetry.
  \item \textbf{GNN-based adversarial (COOBA):} small marginal effect ($d = 0.27$,

\end{itemize}
The CPL paradigm is independently replicated across two architecturally distinct
benchmarked models.
\end{tcolorbox}

\subsection{RQ2: What Project-Level Characteristics Determine CPL Success or Failure?}

\subsubsection{Target Codebase Size}

Target codebase size is the single strongest observable moderator of CPL benefit
in this study: knowing a project's LoC category gives a practitioner a reliable
upper bound on expected gain. Table~\ref{tab:stratified} presents CPL gain stratified
by target codebase size.

For embedding-based models (BLAZE), CPL gain is largest for small-codebase targets
(mean $\Delta$MRR $= +0.179$ for $<$100K LoC) but remains meaningful for all size
groups ($+0.110$ for medium, $+0.106$ for large). Cross-project transfer scales
robustly across the full range of target sizes in this corpus, making BLAZE a safe
choice even for large, complex codebases.

For CNN-based rerankers (TRANP-CNN), the LoC pattern reveals a striking finding:
CP-transfer \emph{exceeds} WP-large for small targets (CPT = 0.532 vs.\

surpasses the within-project performance ceiling. The cross-project initialisation

targets ($+0.025$ MRR); for medium and large targets the gain is smaller than
measurement noise. GNN-based adversarial models fail to generalise their
graph-structured representations to large, architecturally complex codebases even
with source-side pre-training.

\subsubsection{Negative Transfer}

Negative transfer — where source-project knowledge actively harms target performance
— is the central risk in CPL. Understanding when it occurs determines whether CPL
is a safe default or a strategy requiring careful source selection.

We define \emph{negative transfer} as CP-transfer MRR $<$ WP-small MRR by more
than 0.01 points (the neutral band $|\Delta| \leq 0.01$ is treated as no-effect).

For embedding-based models (BLAZE), only 2 of 63 pairs (3.2\%) exhibit negative
transfer under this definition, essentially zero adoption risk. The two cases are
prefecthq/prefect $\to$ ansible/ansible ($-$0.023 MRR) and jupyterlab/jupyterlab
$\to$ localstack/localstack ($-$0.016 MRR) — both cross-domain pairs with large
targets, and both negligible in absolute magnitude.

For CNN-based rerankers (TRANP-CNN), negative transfer affects 17 of 63 pairs
(27.0\%) — roughly one in three pairs. The worst cases include ansible $\to$ prefect
($-$0.094 MRR), jupyterlab $\to$ meson ($-$0.087 MRR), and lightning $\to$ ansible
($-$0.078 MRR). These are concentrated in cross-domain settings where high-specificity
codebases (ansible, prefect, meson) appear as targets.

For GNN-based adversarial models (COOBA), negative transfer affects 20 of 63 pairs
(31.7\%) — nearly a third of all pairs. The worst cases include meson $\to$ jupyterlab
($-$0.152 MRR), qiskit $\to$ jupyterlab ($-$0.084 MRR), and ansible $\to$ jupyterlab
($-$0.080 MRR). The consistent appearance of jupyterlab as the most-harmed target
suggests COOBA's AST-based representations overfit to source-project structural patterns
that conflict with jupyterlab's frontend-heavy codebase.

A 27--32\% negative transfer rate in TRANP-CNN and COOBA means that roughly one in
three CPL deployments will see the model perform worse than if it had only been
trained on limited target data. Practitioners should treat TRANP-CNN and COOBA CPL
as a strategy requiring empirical validation per deployment, not a universally safe
upgrade.

The three-model spectrum (3.2\%$\to$27.0\%$\to$31.7\%) aligns with the win/loss
asymmetry ordering and provides a consistent risk profile for each architectural
paradigm.

Figure \ref{fig:feature_corr} plots Spearman correlations between project-level
metadata features and CPL gain. A key finding is that no single measured feature
reliably predicts CPL success or failure at the pair level for any model class.
Neither LoC ratio ($\rho < 0.15$), domain gap accuracy ($|\rho| < 0.12$), source
bug count ($|\rho| < 0.08$), nor bug report verbosity provides a reliable predictor
when considered in isolation. Negative transfer is not predictable from project
metadata alone, making empirical validation the safest guard against deployment risk.

\subsubsection{Source-Target Domain Alignment}

Table~\ref{tab:crossdomain} presents CPL gain broken down by whether source and
target belong to the same domain (within-domain, Group A pairs) or different domains
(cross-domain, Groups B and C).

For embedding-based models (BLAZE), within-domain pairs gain more on average
($+0.180$ MRR, 93.3\% win rate) than cross-domain pairs ($+0.097$ MRR,
78.8\% win rate), a 14.5 percentage-point drop across domain boundaries.
While this is a notable degradation, cross-domain BLAZE CPL remains strong
in absolute terms (78.8\% win rate), and negative transfer in cross-domain
pairs remains rare. \emph{Domain alignment amplifies CPL benefit for BLAZE
but is not a requirement for it.} Practitioners can attempt cross-domain
CPL with BLAZE but should expect modestly lower win rates than within-domain.

For CNN-based rerankers (TRANP-CNN), domain alignment has a pronounced moderating
effect: within-domain win rate = 76.7\%, cross-domain win rate = 48.5\%, a
28 percentage-point drop that is the steepest of the three models. For TRANP-CNN,
cross-domain pairs fall below the 50\% win rate threshold — CPL is more likely to
hurt than help when source and target belong to different domains. This steep
cross-domain sensitivity confirms that domain gap is the primary risk factor for
CNN-based CPL, and practitioners should prioritise within-domain sources when using
TRANP-CNN-style models.

For GNN-based adversarial models (COOBA), domain alignment has a strikingly
\emph{negligible} effect: within-domain win rate = 43.3\%, cross-domain win
rate = 45.5\%, a difference of only $-$2 percentage points (cross-domain
slightly higher). COOBA's CPL outcome is essentially independent of domain
proximity. This finding contradicts the naive expectation that adversarial
domain adaptation would be most effective within-domain; instead, COOBA's
structural (AST-based) overfitting to the source project determines success or
failure regardless of domain, making domain alignment an unreliable guide for
COOBA source selection.

The domain gap metric, computed as logistic regression accuracy on file embeddings
(Section~\ref{sec:domaingap}), shows no significant correlation with CPL gain for
any model ($|\rho| < 0.12$ for BLAZE and COOBA, all $p > 0.20$; $\rho = 0.11$,
$p = 0.39$ for TRANP-CNN), indicating that continuous embedding-space distance does
not reliably capture the domain alignment that governs TRANP-CNN outcomes.
The domain binary (same vs.\ different application category) is a stronger signal
than the continuous domain gap metric for TRANP-CNN, but a non-signal for COOBA.

\subsubsection{Commutativity: Does Transfer Direction Matter?}

For 28 symmetric pairs (both $A \to B$ and $B \to A$ evaluated), we test whether
the smaller-LoC target consistently gains more from CPL — a hypothesis motivated
by the LoC-stratified results: if smaller codebases benefit more on average, the
same asymmetry should appear within symmetric pairs.

For embedding-based models (BLAZE), 67.9\% of symmetric pairs (19/28) show the
smaller-LoC target gaining more, above the 50\% random baseline, consistent with
the hypothesis that data-scarce targets gain most from external knowledge. For
CNN-based rerankers (TRANP-CNN), the consistency rate is 50.0\% (14/28),
essentially random — LoC asymmetry does not predict transfer direction for
TRANP-CNN; domain alignment and bug distribution are the stronger factors.
For GNN-based adversarial models (COOBA), the consistency rate is 60.7\% (17/28),
a moderate tendency that aligns with the LoC-stratified results showing stronger
CPL gains for small targets. This stands in contrast to TRANP-CNN's random
commutativity (50.0\%), confirming that COOBA's failure mode is LoC-driven even
if its success mode is not domain-driven.

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=gray!10, colframe=gray!50, title=\textbf{RQ2 Summary}]
CPL benefit is largest for small-codebase targets and within-domain pairs, but
neither is a prerequisite for embedding-based models. The three models exhibit a
consistent risk spectrum:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep,topsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{BLAZE:} 3.2\% negative transfer, 14.5pp cross-domain sensitivity
        (moderate), cross-domain CPL still viable at 78.8\% win rate.
  \item \textbf{TRANP-CNN:} 27.0\% negative transfer, 28pp cross-domain sensitivity
        (steepest of the three), CPT exceeds WPL for small targets.
  \item \textbf{COOBA:} 31.7\% negative transfer, LoC-sensitive (gains vanish for
        large targets), no domain sensitivity ($-$2pp, within- and cross-domain
        outcomes are equivalent).
\end{itemize}
No single project-level metadata feature reliably predicts pair-level CPL success
for any model.
\end{tcolorbox}

model's CPL outcome what a different architecture will do on the same pair.

\subsubsection{Source Quality as a Transfer Predictor}

A natural heuristic for source project selection is to choose the source that
performs best within-project. We test this by computing Spearman $\rho$ between
the source project's WP-large MRR and the achieved CP-transfer MRR across all pairs.

For embedding-based models (BLAZE), $\rho = -0.00$ ($p = 0.999$): source within-project
performance has trivially zero predictive power for transfer quality. Practitioners
can freely select source projects without evaluating them within-project first.

For CNN-based rerankers (TRANP-CNN), $\rho = -0.208$ ($p = 0.102$): the direction is
negative but does not reach significance. The practical conclusion mirrors BLAZE:
source within-project performance is not a useful selection criterion.

For GNN-based adversarial models (COOBA), $\rho = -0.296$ ($p = 0.019$): source
within-project performance is a statistically significant \emph{negative} predictor
of transfer quality — sources on which COOBA performs best within-project actually
transfer worse. This counter-intuitive finding arises because COOBA overfits to the
structural patterns of the source when it performs well within-project, making those
patterns a liability rather than an asset when applied to the target.
\emph{Practitioners using COOBA should not use within-project source performance
as a selection criterion; it is actively misleading.}

All three models thus agree on the practical recommendation: source selection
based on within-project quality metrics is unreliable or counter-productive across
all three architectural paradigms.

\subsubsection{Source Selection Heuristics}

Table~\ref{tab:source_selection} summarises the evaluation of two source selection
heuristics — \emph{most-bugs} (select the source with the largest bug corpus) and
\emph{domain-gap} (select the source with the lowest embedding-space domain gap,
i.e.\ the most similar project) — against the oracle upper bound, across all targets
with at least three candidate sources. Hit@1 measures how often the heuristic
selects the oracle-best source; Kendall $\tau$ measures the quality of the full
source ranking; \% oracle measures CP-transfer MRR achieved by the selected source
relative to the best possible source.

For embedding-based models (BLAZE), the most-bugs heuristic achieves Hit@1 = 15.4\%
and $\tau = -0.055$ (near-zero, weakly negative); the domain-gap heuristic does
better on Hit@1 (46.2\%) but shows a similar negative $\tau$ ($-0.192$), meaning
neither heuristic reliably ranks the full source list. Despite this, BLAZE achieves
88.6\%--92.7\% of oracle performance with either heuristic. Source selection has low
stakes for BLAZE; even a poorly chosen source produces near-oracle results.

For CNN-based rerankers (TRANP-CNN), most-bugs achieves Hit@1 = 30.8\% with
$\tau = +0.110$ — the only positive $\tau$ of any heuristic across all three models
— while domain-gap achieves Hit@1 = 23.1\% with $\tau = -0.080$. Oracle recovery
is 86.7\%--89.8\% for both heuristics. The 27.0\% negative transfer risk means
that within-domain validation is advisable regardless of which heuristic is used.

For GNN-based adversarial models (COOBA), most-bugs achieves Hit@1 = 38.5\% with
$\tau = -0.194$; domain-gap achieves Hit@1 = 23.1\% with $\tau \approx 0$
($-0.007$). Both heuristics achieve 78.8\%--79.2\% of oracle performance —
the lowest oracle recovery of the three models. The near-zero $\tau$ for domain-gap
is particularly striking: domain similarity provides essentially no signal for
ranking sources when the CPL mechanism is adversarial domain adaptation, which
already attempts to erase domain-specific features from the learned representations.

The practical takeaway is consistent across both heuristics and all three models:
no tested heuristic reliably ranks sources ($\tau < 0.2$ in all cases), yet all
three models achieve 79--93\% of oracle performance with any heuristic. Practitioners
do not need sophisticated source selection for BLAZE or TRANP-CNN. For COOBA, source
selection matters more (lower absolute oracle recovery) but neither heuristic is
reliable; empirical holdout validation on a small target sample remains the safest
strategy.

\subsection{Cross-Model Synthesis: What the Evidence Says About CPL}
% \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=black!10, colframe=gray!50, title=\textbf{Cross-Model Synthesis: What the Evidence Says About CPL}]
Across all three model architectures and 63 project pairs, the empirical evidence
points to a consistent conclusion: \textbf{cross-project learning is a viable
strategy for data-scarce bug localisation in 2 of 3 architectural paradigms tested,
but its effectiveness and risk profile are architecture-dependent}.

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep,topsep=2pt]
  \item \textit{\textbf{CPL delivers meaningful benefit in 2 of 3 models tested.}}
        Embedding-based models (BLAZE, $d = 1.19$) and CNN-based rerankers
        (TRANP-CNN, $d = 0.59$) both show statistically significant, practically
        substantial CPL benefit across all five evaluation metrics. Only
        the GNN-based adversarial model (COOBA) shows smaller marginal gains
        ($d = 0.27$) that border on practical irrelevance for individual deployments.

  \item \textit{\textbf{Cold-start deployment (zero target annotations) is viable for
        embedding-based models only.}} BLAZE matches within-project limited training
        without any target data in 74.6\% of pairs. Both scalar-ranking models
        (TRANP-CNN and COOBA) require at least some target-side calibration to
        produce useful rankings.

  \item \textit{\textbf{With minimal target fine-tuning, CPL can match or exceed full
        within-project training.}} For BLAZE, CP-transfer significantly outperforms
        WP-small (the practical baseline) with a large effect. For TRANP-CNN,
        CP-transfer (MRR = 0.380) is statistically indistinguishable from WP-large
        (MRR = 0.389, $p = 0.19$), cross-project transfer effectively closes the
        gap to the ample-data ceiling, particularly for small-codebase targets where
        CPT actually exceeds WPL.

  \item \textit{\textbf{Negative transfer is a substantial risk for CNN and GNN models.}}
        Rates are 3.2\% (BLAZE), 27.0\% (TRANP-CNN), and 31.7\% (COOBA) — roughly
        one in three deployments for the latter two. Negative transfer concentrates
        in cross-domain pairs for TRANP-CNN, while COOBA's negative transfer is
        LoC-driven and domain-agnostic. Practitioners should treat TRANP-CNN and
        COOBA CPL as requiring empirical validation, not a universally safe upgrade.

  \item \textit{\textbf{CPL outcome is not predictable from the project pair alone, model architecture is a first-class variable.}} Pairwise cross-model agreement
        ranges from 41\% to 67\%, meaning the same source$\to$target pair can benefit
        one architecture and harm another. Practitioners cannot generalise a CPL
        result from one tool to another without empirical validation.
\end{itemize}
% \end{tcolorbox}

\subsection{Illustrative Cases}

these patterns concrete, we highlight three project pairs that illustrate distinct,
practically important scenarios: cold-start viability, architecture divergence on the

model outperforms 20\% annotated target-only training with zero target labels.
TRANP-CNN and COOBA cold-start both collapse (MRR $= 0.062$ and $0.046$ respectively),
far below their own WPS baselines.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Practical Implications}

The results support four concrete recommendations for practitioners considering
CPL deployment.

\paragraph{1. CPL is a low-risk, high-reward strategy for embedding-based architectures.}
If a project has few labeled bug reports and an embedding-based bug localization
tool is available, cross-project transfer with a source project is beneficial in
90\% of deployment scenarios, with a 27:1 gain-to-loss asymmetry. Critically,
\emph{no target-project annotations are required}: zero-label deployment matches
within-project limited training, eliminating the annotation cost entirely. The
recommendation is to deploy first and annotate later, using the cold-start model
until sufficient target data is collected for fine-tuning.

\paragraph{2. Target codebase size moderates benefit; validate empirically for large targets.}
CPL gain is largest for small-codebase targets ($\Delta$MRR $\approx +0.179$ for
$<$100K LoC) and decreases for larger targets, reaching $\approx +0.106$ for
$>$300K LoC. For GNN-based adversarial models (COOBA), benefits essentially disappear
beyond medium-sized codebases ($+0.006$ MRR for large targets). Practitioners with
large-codebase targets and COOBA-style tools should validate CPL empirically before
relying on it.

\paragraph{3. Source selection has low stakes for BLAZE and TRANP-CNN; use holdout validation for COOBA.}
The most intuitive heuristic (select the source with the most bug reports) fails
to rank sources reliably (Hit@1 = 15.4--38.5\% across models; BLAZE and COOBA have
negative $\tau$, TRANP-CNN has weakly positive $\tau = +0.110$).
Yet all three models achieve 79--89\% of oracle performance regardless of source choice.
Practitioners should select the most conveniently available \emph{within-domain}
source rather than investing in complex selection procedures for BLAZE and TRANP-CNN.
For COOBA, where oracle recovery is lowest (78.8\%) and negative transfer is highest
(31.7\%), empirical holdout validation on a small target sample is advisable.

\paragraph{4. For the highest CPL ceiling, CNN-based rerankers with 20\% target labels match full within-project training.}
When a project has at least 20\% of its bug reports labeled and domain-aligned sources
are available, TRANP-CNN achieves CP-transfer performance statistically indistinguishable
from WP-large ($p = 0.19$), and for small-codebase targets actually exceeds it.
This provides the highest absolute CPL performance of the three models, but carries
27.0\% negative transfer risk concentrated in cross-domain pairs. Practitioners
should validate domain alignment before deploying CNN-based CPL cross-domain.

\subsection{Architectural Interpretation of CPL Profiles}
\label{sec:architecture}

The three models in this study span three distinct paradigms of how a bug
localization model evaluates candidate files at inference time, and the experimental
design was structured to make architectural attribution possible rather than merely
correlational. All three models share the same embedding backbone
(\texttt{BAAI/bge-code-v1}), are evaluated on identical datasets with identical
train/test splits (random seed 42), retrieve candidates from the same FAISS indexes,
and are benchmarked across the same 63 project pairs. Representation quality,
training data access, retrieval candidates, and pair selection are all held constant
across the three model evaluations. The only free variable distinguishing the three CPL profiles is
each model's \textbf{evaluation mechanism}, what its learned parameters do to
rank candidates at test time. This controlled design is what allows the performance
differences to be attributed to architectural choices rather than to confounding
experimental conditions.

The divergent CPL profiles map cleanly onto that mechanism.

\paragraph{BLAZE (embedding-based dual-encoder).}
BLAZE scores candidate files at inference time using cosine similarity between
frozen BGE backbone embeddings. No learned scalar weight is applied at ranking
time: the ordering is determined entirely by pre-trained representations built on a
diverse multilingual code corpus that already carry cross-project semantics before
any fine-tuning begins. Cold-start viability follows directly from this mechanism,
there is nothing project-specific to calibrate, because no target-specific ranking
weights exist. The 27:1 gain-to-loss asymmetry follows from the same property:
similarity-based ranking is inherently smooth. A suboptimal source project produces
suboptimal but not catastrophically wrong cosine similarities, bounding loss
magnitude; gains from well-aligned source-target embedding spaces can be large.
Source selection therefore has low stakes: even a poorly chosen source project produces
rankings that are only marginally off.

A note on two competing hypotheses. \textit{(i) Frozen backbone}: BLAZE differs
from COOBA and TRANP-CNN in that its BGE backbone is \emph{frozen} during training
(adapter-only fine-tuning), while COOBA and TRANP-CNN fine-tune projection layers
on top of BGE features. One might ask whether BLAZE's superior CPL profile is
driven by frozen backbone expressivity rather than its evaluation mechanism.
\textit{(ii) Model age}: BLAZE (2025) is four to six years newer than COOBA (2021)
and TRANP-CNN (2019); one might ask whether the performance gap reflects progress
in pre-trained representations rather than architectural differences.
This second concern is addressed by the shared backbone design: all three models
receive the same \texttt{BAAI/bge-code-v1} embeddings as input, equalising absolute
representation quality; any performance difference arises in how each model uses
those embeddings at ranking time, not in the quality of embeddings they receive.
The frozen-backbone hypothesis is addressed as follows: Two observations make the
evaluation-mechanism account more parsimonious. First, if frozen backbone were the
key factor, cold-start performance should be near-optimal even without adapter
training, but CP-cold-start achieves only MRR\,=\,0.262 vs.\ CP-transfer
MRR\,=\,0.391, indicating that adapter fine-tuning on source data still provides
meaningful signal. Second, the 27:1 gain-to-loss \emph{asymmetry}, the ratio of
expected gain to expected loss when CPL does not help, is a property of ranking
smoothness, not of backbone capacity: a frozen backbone can produce arbitrarily bad
rankings if source and target embedding distributions are misaligned, but it produces
smooth, bounded losses by virtue of the cosine similarity's range. Both effects
point to the evaluation mechanism as the primary driver, though the frozen-backbone
and evaluation-mechanism explanations are not mutually exclusive.

\paragraph{COOBA (GNN with adversarial domain adaptation).}
COOBA trains a Gradient Reversal Layer discriminator to erase project-specific
signals from the shared GNN feature space. This adversarial objective is the CPL
mechanism: by preventing the discriminator from distinguishing source from target
representations, the encoder is forced to produce domain-invariant features.
However, the mechanism requires target-side representations \emph{during training}
so the discriminator has something to contrast against the source distribution.
With zero target data, the discriminator receives no target signal; the resulting
features remain calibrated to the source AST vocabulary. Because AST vocabularies (identifier namespaces, call-graph structures, and type hierarchies) are
project-specific, the features misalign when applied to a new target, explaining
the near-zero cold-start MRR ($0.027$ vs.\ $0.138$ for WP-small). The
counter-intuitive finding that higher source within-project performance predicts
\emph{worse} transfer follows from the same logic: a model that has learned source
AST patterns very precisely has encoded more source-specific features, which
generalise less to a new project's AST vocabulary.

\paragraph{TRANP-CNN (CNN reranker).}
TRANP-CNN produces a scalar relevance score for each (bug report, candidate file)
pair via parallel convolutional feature extractors and a margin ranking loss.
This scalar is calibrated during training to the ranking difficulty of the source
project's file distribution. Without target examples, the score function remains
anchored to the source distribution; its scale and decision boundary do not transfer
to the target, explaining cold-start failure (MRR = 0.076 vs.\ WP-small 0.328).
With 20\% target fine-tuning (CP-transfer), TRANP-CNN benefits from source
pre-training with recalibration to the target's ranking difficulty, producing a
medium-effect CPL profile ($d = 0.59$, *** across all metrics) intermediate between
COOBA ($d = 0.27$) and BLAZE ($d = 1.19$). Notably, this recalibrated CP-transfer
result is statistically indistinguishable from WP-large ($p = 0.19$), confirming
that source pre-training provides a quality of initialisation that closes the gap
to the ample-data ceiling once target calibration is provided.

A particularly important cross-check on the architectural interpretation is the
chronological ordering of the three models. COOBA (2021) employs a more
sophisticated adversarial adaptation mechanism than TRANP-CNN (2019), yet achieves
substantially worse CPL outcomes across nearly all metrics. If model age or
methodological sophistication were the explanatory variable, COOBA should outperform
TRANP-CNN, it does not. This rules out publication year and architectural
complexity as confounds, leaving the evaluation mechanism as the consistent
explanatory dimension. Specifically: whether ranking at inference time requires
target-side calibration of learned parameters determines CPL viability. Models
that rank using pre-trained representation similarity (BLAZE) do not require
calibration and can operate in cold-start; models that rank using a learned scalar
(TRANP-CNN, COOBA) do require calibration and fail in cold-start. This
inference-time mechanism is independent of training-time sophistication.

\subsection{Implications for CPL Research}

The low cross-model agreement (41--67\% across the three model pairs) is a
methodological contribution for the CPL research community. It establishes that
CPL benefit cannot be assumed to generalise across model architectures: a finding
from one CPL model does not reliably predict outcomes from a different architecture
on the same project pairs. Future
CPL model papers should evaluate against the limited within-project baseline
(WP-small) rather than, or in addition to, within-project ample-data baselines.
Reporting results against WP-large alone creates an inflated impression of CPL
benefit by comparing against a standard that data-scarce practitioners cannot reach.

The counter-intuitive negative predictor for source quality in GNN-based adversarial
models (COOBA), better within-project performance predicts worse transfer,
suggests that within-project performance and cross-project generalisability may
be competing objectives for this architecture class. High within-project performance
may reflect overfitting to source-specific AST patterns that do not generalise.
This motivates CPL-specific model design that explicitly trades within-project
peak performance for cross-project robustness, an open research direction not
addressed by existing proposals. Notably, this tension does not appear in
CNN-based or embedding-based rerankers, where source selection is essentially
irrelevant to transfer outcome.

\subsection{Threats to Validity}

\paragraph{Internal validity.}
All three models depend on FAISS retrieval at the first stage. FAISS recall at
$K = 300$ varies substantially across the 13 projects in this study: from 31.3\%
(ansible) to 100\% (xarray), with a median of approximately 70\%. Three projects
fall below 50\% recall: ansible (31.3\%), wagtail (34.6\%), and meson (45.5\%),
meaning that for these projects the reranking model never sees the ground-truth
file in roughly half to two-thirds of queries. Ground-truth files not retrieved
within the top-300 candidates are automatically missed by the reranking model,
imposing a shared performance ceiling that suppresses absolute localisation metrics.
The large-codebase performance degradation observed in Table~\ref{tab:stratified}
therefore conflates model architectural limitation with retrieval ceiling; disentangling
the two would require re-ranking-only evaluation over gold-standard candidate sets
and is left to future work. This ceiling does not threaten the within-model CPL
comparison (CPT vs.\ WPS), where both conditions face the same retrieval constraint. All train/test splits use a
single fixed random seed (42); results could vary under different seeds, though
with 63 source$\to$target pairs per model, the aggregated statistics are unlikely
to be seed-sensitive.

\paragraph{Construct validity.}
MRR and Top-$K$ measure file-level localisation. Finer-grained (function- or
line-level) localisation may show different transfer patterns if fine-grained
labels are available and the model architecture exploits them. The current
evaluation is aligned with the benchmarks used in prior CPL literature.
Additionally, the BLAZE architecture uses a frozen BGE backbone (adapter-only
fine-tuning), whereas TRANP-CNN and COOBA fine-tune projection layers on top of
BGE features. This asymmetry in backbone treatment is a design property of the
original model architectures rather than an experimental choice; it is a potential
source of differential expressivity that readers should consider when interpreting
the large effect-size gap between BLAZE and the other two models.

\paragraph{External validity.}
The study covers 13 Python open-source projects. Generalisability to other
programming languages, proprietary codebases, or projects with different
bug-report vocabularies and styles is empirically unvalidated. The Python scope
is by design: cross-language CPL introduces additional syntactic and semantic
variation that is a distinct research question deferred to future work.
The mechanism-based explanations in Section~\ref{sec:architecture} are grounded
in inference-time properties (similarity-based vs.\ scalar-ranking) that are
language-agnostic in principle; however, COOBA's AST-based encoder is sensitive
to the structure of the AST vocabulary — identifier namespaces, call-graph depth,
type hierarchies — which differs substantially between Python and Java. Whether
the COOBA CPL failure mode (near-zero cold-start, LoC-driven ceiling) replicates
in Java, where ASTs are more verbose and richly type-annotated, is an open
empirical question that the present study cannot answer.
Additionally, only open-source projects with publicly accessible commit histories
are included; proprietary codebases may have different bug-report characteristics
or code organisation patterns. The embedding backbone (\texttt{BAAI/bge-code-v1})
is specific to the version used at evaluation time; a different or updated backbone
may produce different absolute numbers while leaving the relative architectural
conclusions intact.

\paragraph{Conclusion validity.}
The cross-model consistency finding is based on the shared pairs evaluated by all
three models. TRANP-CNN represents a third architectural data point with a medium effect ($d = 0.59$),
intermediate between the large-effect embedding-based (BLAZE, $d = 1.19$) and
small-effect GNN-based adversarial results (COOBA, $d = 0.27$).
The Wilcoxon tests for each model are conducted independently per metric; the
Bonferroni correction applied within each test family controls the familywise
error rate across five metrics per model. The 63-pair set is a structured rather
than random sample of the 156 possible directed pairs: it over-represents DS$\times$DS
pairs (Group A, 30 pairs) relative to the full pair distribution. Aggregate statistics
over all 63 pairs should be interpreted in light of this design choice, though the
stratified analysis in RQ2 (Tables~\ref{tab:stratified} and~\ref{tab:crossdomain})
isolates within-domain from cross-domain effects.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Bug localization models require labeled training data that newly started or
data-scarce projects often cannot provide. Cross-project learning has been proposed
as a solution, but no existing work has systematically compared CPL against the
practically relevant baseline of within-project limited training, nor tested whether
CPL findings hold across different model implementations.

We conducted a controlled empirical study across 63 source$\to$target project pairs,
13 open-source Python projects, and three state-of-the-art bug localization models
under four experimental scenarios designed to isolate the contribution of cross-project
knowledge.

Our answers to the three research questions are:

\textbf{RQ1.} Cross-project transfer significantly outperforms within-project limited
training for 2 of 3 architectural paradigms: embedding-based ($d = 1.19$, win rate
90.5\%, $p < 0.001$) and CNN-based reranker ($d = 0.59$, win rate 68.3\%, $p < 0.001$).
Zero-label deployment matches within-project limited training for embedding-based
models, eliminating annotation cost in 74.6\% of pairs. CNN-based rerankers uniquely
achieve CP-transfer performance statistically tied with full within-project training
($p = 0.19$). GNN-based adversarial models show only marginal benefit ($d \approx 0.27$,
$p < 0.05$) with near-zero cold-start performance.

\textbf{RQ2.} CPL benefit is largest for small-codebase targets and within-domain
pairs, but neither is a prerequisite for embedding-based models. The three models
exhibit a consistent risk spectrum: negative transfer rates of 3.2\% (BLAZE),
27.0\% (TRANP-CNN), and 31.7\% (COOBA) — roughly one in three deployments for the
latter two architectures. Domain sensitivity is steepest for CNN-based rerankers
(28pp win-rate drop cross-domain); GNN-based adversarial models show no domain
effect, reflecting architecture-driven rather than domain-driven limitations. No
single project-level metadata feature reliably predicts pair-level CPL success for
any model.

\textbf{RQ3.} CPL outcomes are architecture-dependent: cross-model direction
agreement ranges from 41\% (BLAZE vs.\ COOBA) to 67\% (BLAZE vs.\ TRANP-CNN).
Source within-project quality does not predict transfer success for any model and
is a negative predictor for COOBA (a source that works well within-project tends
to transfer poorly). Two heuristics tested (most-bugs and domain-gap) both fail to
reliably rank sources ($|\tau| < 0.2$ for all model-heuristic combinations), yet
all three models achieve 79--93\% of oracle performance regardless of source choice,
making heuristic source ranking low-priority for BLAZE and TRANP-CNN.

The broader significance of these findings is that data-scarce projects no longer
need to wait years to accumulate sufficient labeled data before deploying bug
localization. The deployment pathways revealed by this study are concrete:
embedding-based tools can be applied immediately using any within-domain source
project with zero target annotations; CNN-based rerankers require only 20\% of
target labels and then match the performance achievable with full within-project
training. The replication of CPL benefit across two independently designed
architectures (BLAZE and TRANP-CNN) strengthens the evidence beyond any single-model
study, establishing CPL as a reliable paradigm rather than an artefact of a
particular model's inductive bias. The architecture-dependence of CPL outcomes,
and specifically the failure of GNN-based adversarial models to reliably benefit from
CPL, establishes that architectural choice is a first-class variable in CPL
deployment, motivating empirical validation for each architectural choice before
production use, and informing future CPL model design that explicitly targets
cross-project robustness at inference time.

Future work will: (a) extend the study to multi-language CPL (Java, Python
cross-language pairs, and Kotlin/Go) to test whether findings generalise beyond
Python; (b) develop model-specific source selection criteria that go beyond project
metadata, such as embedding-space proximity between source and target corpora;
(c) evaluate CPL at function and line level for architectures that support
fine-grained localisation; and (d) investigate whether the CPT$\approx$WPL ceiling
for CNN-based rerankers can be matched by GNN-based models through modified training
objectives that decouple within-project and cross-project performance.
