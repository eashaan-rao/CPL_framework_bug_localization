%% ABSTRACT
%% ===================================================================
\begin{abstract}
Bug localization models require paired training data --- bug reports and their
ground-truth responsible source files --- that newly started or data-scarce projects
cannot readily supply. Cross-project learning (CPL) transfers knowledge from a
donor project to address this scarcity, but existing work evaluates CPL against
within-project ample-data baselines rather than against the limited data a
data-scarce target already has. Whether CPL findings generalise across model
implementations is also unknown.

We empirically evaluate whether CPL outperforms training on a small portion of
the target project's own data. We study 63 source$\to$target project pairs across
13 open-source Python projects spanning four software domains, using three
state-of-the-art bug localization models and four experimental scenarios that
control target-project supervision from zero to 80\% of available labels.

Cross-project transfer with minimal target labels significantly outperforms
within-project limited training for embedding-based models across all five
evaluation metrics (win rate: 90.3\%, Cohen's $d = 1.20$, $p < 0.001$). Applying
a source-trained model directly to a new project without any target annotations
matches within-project limited training in 74.2\% of evaluated pairs, eliminating
annotation cost in most deployment scenarios. CPL benefit is moderated by target
codebase size, source-target domain alignment, and model implementation: for
structure-based models, gains are small ($d = 0.28$) and negative transfer
occurs in 24.2\% of pairs. Cross-model direction agreement on CPL benefit is
only 41\% across shared pairs.

CPL is a viable and low-risk substitute for scarce within-project training data
when using embedding-based architectures. The benefit is not universal and is
model-dependent, motivating empirical validation before practitioner adoption.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Bug localization --- automatically identifying the source files responsible for a
reported defect --- is a critical and labour-intensive task in software maintenance.
Modern deep learning-based bug localization models achieve strong performance on
established benchmarks \citep{lam2017bug,liang2022modeling,ciborowska2022fast}, but
their effectiveness depends on one prerequisite that is easy to overlook: they require
substantial labeled training data, namely paired examples of bug reports and the
corresponding ground-truth files that were modified to resolve each bug. In practice,
projects accumulate this data only through extended active development. Newly
open-sourced codebases, projects migrating from proprietary issue trackers, or systems
with low bug-filing rates may have only dozens to a few hundred labeled bug reports ---
far fewer than models typically require for effective within-project training.

Cross-project learning (CPL) has been proposed as a solution to this scarcity:
train a bug localization model on bug reports and source code from a donor source
project, then apply or fine-tune it on the target project \citep{huo2019deep,zhu2020cooba,chakraborty2025blaze}.
The intuition is that the relationship between natural-language defect descriptions
and their corresponding code locations may transfer across projects that share a
programming language, coding style, or functional domain. Several CPL models have been
proposed and evaluated in controlled settings, demonstrating the feasibility of
knowledge transfer \citep{huo2019deep,zhu2020cooba,zhu2022bl,chakraborty2025blaze}.
However, a critical gap remains: existing work predominantly evaluates CPL against
within-project training \emph{with ample data} --- the ideal scenario that a
data-scarce practitioner cannot realistically achieve.

This distinction is consequential for practitioners. The question ``does CPL
outperform within-project training with 10,000 labeled examples?'' is not the
question a newly-deploying team actually needs answered. Their question is:
``I have 100 labeled bug reports for my project --- should I use them for within-project
training, or instead borrow from a related project?'' No existing study provides
systematic empirical evidence to answer this question. Furthermore, prior CPL work
evaluates individual models in isolation; whether CPL benefit is a property of the
paradigm or an artefact of a specific model's inductive biases remains unknown,
limiting practitioners' ability to generalise findings across tool choices.

We fill this gap with a controlled empirical study of CPL viability. We evaluate
three state-of-the-art bug localization models across 63 directional source$\to$target
project pairs drawn from 13 open-source Python projects spanning four software domains
(Data Science/ML, Developer Tools, Systems/Cloud, and Applications). For each pair we
measure performance under four experimental scenarios designed to isolate the
contribution of cross-project knowledge while controlling for the amount of
target-project supervision. Crucially, the primary baseline is always \emph{within-project
limited training} --- the data a newly active project actually has --- rather than
within-project ample data.

Our results answer the practitioner's question with empirical evidence. Cross-project
transfer with minimal target labels consistently and significantly outperforms limited
within-project training for embedding-based models, with a large effect detectable
across all five evaluation metrics. Remarkably, a source-trained model applied
directly to a new target project --- with zero annotations from that target ---
achieves equivalent performance to within-project limited training in the majority of
evaluated pairs, suggesting that annotation effort can be eliminated entirely in many
deployment scenarios. CPL benefit is largest for small-codebase targets and persists
across source-target domain combinations. However, CPL benefit is not guaranteed: it
varies significantly with model implementation, and one of the three models studied
shows frequent performance degradation under cross-project transfer, with cross-model
direction agreement of only 41\% across shared pairs.

The contributions of this work are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item The first systematic empirical comparison of CPL against the
        practically relevant baseline of within-project limited training, across
        63 source$\to$target pairs and 13 projects.
  \item Evidence that zero-label cross-project deployment (CP-cold-start) is
        viable for embedding-based bug localization, matching within-project
        limited training without any target annotations.
  \item Characterisation of the conditions --- target codebase size, domain
        alignment, and model implementation --- under which CPL is most and
        least effective, including identification of the negative transfer risk
        profile for each model class.
  \item A cross-model consistency analysis establishing that CPL benefit is
        model-dependent, not a universal property of the paradigm, and that
        source quality on the donor project does not reliably predict transfer
        success.
\end{itemize}

The rest of the paper is organised as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews
related work on bug localization and CPL. Section~\ref{sec:design} describes the
study design. Section~\ref{sec:results} presents results for each research question.
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses practical implications and threats to validity.
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Bug Localization}

Bug localization is the task of ranking source files (or functions) by their
likelihood of containing the defect described in a natural-language bug report.
Early approaches relied on information-retrieval similarity between bug report text
and source file content \citep{saha2013improving,ye2014learning}, using measures
such as BM25 or VSM over code tokens. These methods are simple and require no
training data, but their ranking quality is limited by vocabulary mismatch between
bug report prose and code identifiers.

Deep learning approaches substantially improved on IR baselines by learning
joint representations of bug reports and code. \citet{lam2017bug} (DNNLOC)
introduced a multi-layer neural network that combines textual similarity, file
change history, and structural features, achieving significant improvements over
IR on Java benchmarks. \citet{liang2022modeling} extended this to function-level
localisation using a graph neural network that captures call dependencies.
\citet{ciborowska2022fast} replaced conventional encoders with pre-trained BERT
representations over code changesets, demonstrating that transfer from large
pre-trained models reduces the labeled-data requirement. A recent survey by
\citet{niu2025deep} covers the full landscape of deep learning approaches and
confirms that labeled within-project data remains the standard assumption across
this literature. The benchmark of \citet{lee2018bench4bl} provides standardised
evaluation corpora covering 51 Java projects, and has become a reference point
for comparing bug localization methods.

A consistent limitation of all within-project approaches is that they assume
the target project has accumulated sufficient labeled bug reports for training.
This assumption fails for new or data-scarce projects, which is the exact
regime that motivates the present study.

\subsection{Cross-Project Bug Localization}

Cross-project bug localization (CPL) addresses data scarcity by transferring
knowledge from a source project to a target. \citet{huo2019deep} proposed
TRANP-CNN, the first deep learning CPL model: a convolutional network that
learns shared representations of bug-report--code pairs from a source project
and applies them to a target. \citet{zhu2020cooba} introduced COOBA, which
uses adversarial domain adaptation \citep{ganin2016domain} via a gradient
reversal layer to learn domain-invariant representations from AST-structured
code embeddings. \citet{zhu2022bl} proposed BL-GAN, a semi-supervised approach
using generative adversarial networks to augment scarce target labels.
\citet{chakraborty2025blaze} proposed BLAZE, a dual-encoder contrastive model
using dynamic chunking and hard-negative mining that evaluates cross-project and
cross-language transfer.

Despite this body of work, a critical gap remains: each paper proposes and
evaluates its own model, typically comparing against within-project ample-data
baselines or earlier CPL methods. \emph{No existing study} (i) uses multiple CPL
models as independent instruments, (ii) evaluates against the practitioner-relevant
limited within-project baseline, (iii) characterises the conditions governing CPL
success at scale, or (iv) tests cross-model consistency of CPL findings.

\subsection{Transfer Learning in Software Engineering}

The broader literature on cross-project transfer in software engineering provides
useful context. Cross-project defect prediction --- predicting whether a software
module will be defective, using training data from another project --- has been
studied systematically over many years. \citet{hosseini2017systematic} survey
90 such studies and conclude that cross-project transfer is context-dependent:
it helps in some conditions and hurts in others, with no single factor reliably
predicting transfer success across all settings. This finding from the defect
prediction literature directly motivates our characterisation of the conditions
governing CPL success in bug localisation, and our focus on negative transfer
as a deployment risk. The methodological parallel is informative: like defect
prediction, bug localisation is a within-project learning task where CPL is
possible but not guaranteed to be beneficial.

The theoretical basis for adversarial domain adaptation used in COOBA is
domain-adversarial neural networks \citep{ganin2016domain}, which minimise a
feature-level divergence between source and target distributions by training a
domain discriminator whose gradients are reversed during feature extraction.
This technique has been applied in a number of software engineering transfer
learning settings, but its effectiveness for cross-project code understanding
depends critically on whether the feature extractor captures representations that
are genuinely domain-invariant --- an assumption we test empirically.

\subsection{Embedding-Based Code Representations}

Recent advances in pre-trained code embeddings have substantially raised the
quality of representations available to both within-project and cross-project
models. The \texttt{BAAI/bge-code-v1} model \citep{bge_code} --- a 1536-dimensional
Qwen2-based decoder pre-trained on a diverse multilingual code corpus --- provides
the embedding backbone for all three models in this study, ensuring that representation
quality is not a confound when comparing architectures. The high-dimensional, semantically
rich embeddings produced by this model are sufficient to support dense retrieval
(FAISS inner-product search) that achieves 100\% recall on the target file within
top-300 candidates across all 13 projects. This finding, reported in Section~\ref{sec:results},
establishes that embedding quality is not the performance bottleneck, focusing
attention on the reranking and adaptation stages where model-architectural differences
are consequential.

In contrast to prior CPL work that evaluates single models in controlled settings,
this study (i) uses three architecturally distinct models as independent instruments,
(ii) employs the practitioner-relevant limited within-project baseline as the
primary comparator, (iii) characterises the conditions governing CPL success
across 63 project pairs, and (iv) tests cross-model consistency of the CPL finding.

\section{Study Design}
\label{sec:design}

\subsection{Research Questions}

This study is organised around three research questions, each targeting a distinct
aspect of CPL viability.

\begin{description}
  \item[\textbf{RQ1}] \emph{Does cross-project learning outperform within-project
    training under label scarcity?} This tests the core CPL premise against the
    practitioner-relevant baseline: a project that has only a small number of
    labeled bug reports. Unlike prior work that compares CPL to ample within-project
    data, we test whether CPL adds value over the data the target project already has.

  \item[\textbf{RQ2}] \emph{What project-level characteristics determine whether CPL
    succeeds or fails?} Even if CPL is beneficial on average, practitioners need to
    know \emph{when} it is and is not appropriate. This question identifies moderating
    conditions (target size, domain alignment) and quantifies the risk of negative
    transfer.

  \item[\textbf{RQ3}] \emph{How consistent are CPL outcomes across different model
    implementations?} If CPL benefit is a property of the paradigm, outcomes should
    be consistent across architectures. If it is model-specific, practitioners
    cannot generalise findings from one tool to another. This question also
    evaluates whether project metadata reliably guides source project selection.
\end{description}

\subsection{Benchmarked Models}
\label{sec:models}

A central design choice is to evaluate CPL using three architecturally distinct
bug localization models rather than a single representative. This guards against
conclusions that are artefacts of one model's inductive bias. The three models
span the dominant paradigms in the current literature.

\paragraph{BLAZE \citep{chakraborty2025blaze}.}
BLAZE is a dual-encoder contrastive model that ranks \emph{all} source files
in the target project directly, without a FAISS pre-filter stage. Its backbone is
\texttt{BAAI/bge-code-v1} \citep{bge_code}, frozen during training. Two modal-specific
residual adapter modules --- one for bug reports (prefixed \texttt{search\_query:}) and
one for code chunks (prefixed \texttt{search\_document:}) --- are trained with
in-batch NTXentLoss ($\tau = 0.3$) using hard-negative and hard-positive scaling.
At evaluation time, each source file is scored as the maximum cosine similarity
over its 512-token chunks (MaxSim aggregation). This architecture is sensitive to
the most relevant passage within a long file, which is advantageous for large
codebases.

\paragraph{COOBA \citep{zhu2020cooba}.}
COOBA is a graph neural network model that encodes source files as Abstract Syntax
Tree (AST) graphs processed by graph convolutional layers (GCNConv) with global
mean pooling. Bug reports are encoded by a bidirectional LSTM over GloVe-300 token
embeddings. A shared BGE-based extractor produces domain-invariant representations
via a Gradient Reversal Layer \citep{ganin2016domain} placed before a binary project
discriminator: the shared extractor is simultaneously optimised to minimise ranking
loss and to prevent the discriminator from distinguishing source from target project
representations. This adversarial objective ($\lambda_\mathrm{adv} = 0.1$) is the
core CPL mechanism. COOBA uses FAISS top-300 retrieval as a first stage.

\paragraph{TRANP-CNN \citep{huo2019deep}.}
TRANP-CNN is a CNN-based reranker that processes (bug report, candidate file) pairs
through parallel convolutional feature extractors (kernel sizes 3, 4, 5) and produces
scalar relevance scores. It is trained with a margin ranking loss on FAISS top-300
candidates. As TRANP-CNN results cover only 8 pairs (a DS$\times$DS subset completed
to date), they are treated as directional evidence only; statistical conclusions for
this model are noted as underpowered.

encoding (COOBA), and convolutional text-code alignment (TRANP-CNN), providing

\subsection{Dataset Selection and Curation}
\label{sec:dataset}

\paragraph{Source Benchmarks.}
The candidate pool was assembled by consolidating projects from five established
bug localization benchmarks: \textbf{Bench4BL} \citep{lee2018bench4bl},
\textbf{BeetleBox} \citep{chakraborty2025blaze}, \textbf{LCA} \citep{bogomolov2024long},
\textbf{SWE-bench} \citep{jimenez2023swe}, and the dataset of \citet{ye2014learning}.
Drawing from multiple sources avoids the selection bias of any single benchmark.
The initial pool comprised over 500 unique repositories across multiple languages.

\paragraph{Filtering Criteria.}
Projects were filtered by language-stratified thresholds ensuring statistically
adequate bug report volumes. Python and Java projects required at least 100 unique
bug reports with verified ground-truth file mappings (commit SHAs); under-represented
languages required at least 20. Reports with missing annotations, ambiguous multi-file
patches ($> 10$ files), or non-source patches (documentation, CI configuration) were
discarded. This produced a curated pool of 98 repositories (41 Python, 34 Java, 9
Kotlin, 6 JavaScript, 5 C++, 3 Go).

\paragraph{Phase 1 Scope: 13 Python Projects.}
For Phase 1 we restrict to \textbf{13 Python projects} to establish a
language-homogeneous baseline, avoiding confounds from cross-language syntactic
differences. The 13 projects satisfy three diversity criteria simultaneously:
(i) \emph{codebase size}: 35K--572K lines of code; (ii) \emph{domain}: four
distinct application domains; (iii) \emph{bug corpus size}: 110--1,336 bug reports.

\subsection{Data Preprocessing}
\label{sec:preprocessing}

The preprocessing pipeline runs once per project and persists outputs as
project-level databases, avoiding redundant recomputation across the 756 experiment
runs.

\paragraph{Bug Report Curation.}
Raw bug reports are extracted from the five benchmarks and cross-referenced against
repository commit history to obtain ground-truth file mappings. Each report is
uniquely identified by its commit SHA. Reports with missing SHAs, ambiguous patches
($>10$ files), or non-source patches are discarded. The resulting bug metadata
database contains \texttt{\{bug\_id, bug\_text, gt\_file\_path, commit\_sha\}}.

\paragraph{Source Code Embedding.}
All source files are extracted at the commit corresponding to each bug report
(project state at filing time). Files are encoded with \textbf{BAAI/bge-code-v1}
\citep{bge_code}, a 1536-dimensional Qwen2-based decoder pre-trained for code
understanding. Files exceeding 512 tokens are split into overlapping chunks
(\textbf{512 tokens, 50-token overlap}); chunk embeddings are averaged to produce
a single file-level \texttt{float32} vector. Bug report texts are encoded analogously.
The pipeline uses producer-consumer multiprocessing (8 CPU workers) with batched
GPU inference (batch size 256), processing all 13 projects in approximately
6 hours on a single A100 GPU.

Per-project outputs are two binary databases: a \emph{blob embedding database}
mapping each source file's blob SHA to its 1536-dimensional embedding, and a
\emph{bug metadata database} mapping each bug ID to its embedding and ground-truth
annotation. All three models use these same databases, ensuring that any observed
performance differences are attributable to model architecture rather than input
representation quality.

\subsection{Evaluation Pipeline}
\label{sec:pipeline}

\paragraph{TRANP-CNN and COOBA: Retriever-Reranker.}
Both models use a two-stage pipeline. In the retrieval stage, a \textbf{FAISS
IndexFlatIP} index is built from L2-normalised file embeddings (inner product =
cosine similarity). Each bug report is encoded, normalised, and queried to retrieve
the top-$K = 300$ candidates. In the reranking stage, the respective model processes
each (bug report, candidate) pair and re-sorts by relevance score. Evaluation
metrics are computed over the re-sorted list.

\paragraph{BLAZE: Full-Scan Dual-Encoder.}
BLAZE bypasses the FAISS retrieval stage: at evaluation time it scores every file in
the target codebase directly via cosine similarity between the bug report embedding
and each chunk embedding (MaxSim aggregation). Performance is therefore not bounded
by any retrieval ceiling.

\subsection{Training Scenarios}
\label{sec:scenarios}

Four complementary training regimes isolate specific components of the CPL hypothesis.
The test set (20\% of target bug reports, seed 42) is held out across all scenarios;
only the training data differs.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{WP-small} (Within-Project, Limited): Model trained on approximately
    20\% of the target bug corpus. Represents the data a newly active project
    actually has, and serves as the \emph{primary baseline} for all CPL comparisons.

  \item \textbf{WP-large} (Within-Project, Ample): Model trained on 80\% of the
    target bug corpus. The performance ceiling achievable with maximum within-project
    supervision.

  \item \textbf{CP-cold-start} (Cross-Project, Zero-Shot): Model trained on 100\%
    of the source project's bug reports, with zero target data. Tests zero-label
    deployment viability.

  \item \textbf{CP-transfer} (Cross-Project, Fine-Tuned): Model trained on 100\%
    of source bugs plus approximately 20\% of target bugs. The core CPL scenario,
    deliberately matched in target-data budget to WP-small for a fair comparison.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}

Five complementary metrics provide a complete picture of retrieval breadth and
ranking precision, computed over the re-ranked candidate list for each test bug
report and averaged over all test reports.

\textbf{Top-$K$ Recall} ($K \in \{1, 5, 10\}$): fraction of bug reports for which
the ground-truth file appears within the top $K$ positions. Top-1 captures
precision-at-one; Top-10 captures shortlist breadth.

\textbf{Mean Average Precision (MAP)}: in the single-ground-truth setting used here,
MAP reduces to $1/r_i$ averaged over all bug reports $i$, where $r_i$ is the rank
of the correct file.

\textbf{Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR)}: mean of $1/\text{rank}$ across all bug reports.
Identical to MAP in the single-relevant-document case; both are reported for
comparability with prior literature.

MRR and MAP diverge from Top-$K$ when the correct file is retrieved but ranked
outside the top-$K$ shortlist. This divergence is itself informative: it reveals
whether failures are catastrophic (file not retrieved) or marginal (file ranked
too low).

\subsection{Domain Gap Measurement}
\label{sec:domaingap}

To quantify structural dissimilarity between source and target projects, a
\textbf{domain gap score} is computed for every pair by training a binary logistic
regression classifier to distinguish the two projects' file embeddings. Procedure:
(i) all file-level BGE embeddings from the source project are assigned label 0 and
from the target project label 1; (ii) a balanced 80/20 stratified split is
constructed; (iii) a \texttt{LogisticRegression} classifier with $\ell_2$
regularisation (default $C = 1.0$, up to 1000 iterations) is trained and evaluated
on the held-out split; (iv) the test accuracy is recorded as the domain gap score.

A score of 0.5 indicates that the two projects are indistinguishable in embedding
space (no domain shift); a score of 1.0 indicates fully disjoint embedding regions
(maximum domain shift). The linear probe approach has been widely used as a
separability diagnostic in the domain adaptation literature \citep{ganin2016domain}
and avoids the risk of a more powerful classifier overfitting on small embedding
datasets. Domain gap scores for all pairs are computed once and shared across all
three model evaluations.

\subsection{Statistical Analysis Methods}
\label{sec:statmethods}

\paragraph{RQ1 --- Wilcoxon Signed-Rank Test.}
To assess whether CP-transfer outperforms WP-small, a \textbf{Wilcoxon signed-rank
test} \citep{wilcoxon1945} is applied to each metric separately. This non-parametric
paired test is appropriate because: observations are naturally paired (each
source$\to$target pair contributes one CPT and one WPS result under identical test
conditions); and the distribution of improvements cannot be assumed normal across
such a diverse corpus. A one-sided alternative is tested ($H_1$: CPT $>$ WPS).
Effect sizes are reported as Cohen's $d$ \citep{cohen1988statistical}:
$d \geq 0.8$ large, $\geq 0.5$ medium, $\geq 0.2$ small.

Significance thresholds: $\star\star\star$ ($p < 0.001$), $\star\star$ ($p < 0.01$),
$\star$ ($p < 0.05$), n.s. ($p \geq 0.05$). Bonferroni correction is applied where
multiple comparisons are made within a single test family.

\paragraph{RQ2 --- Spearman Rank Correlation.}
To identify project-level features predictive of CPL gain, \textbf{Spearman's $\rho$}
is computed between each feature and CPT$-$WPS gain across all pairs. Spearman's
$\rho$ is preferred over Pearson's $r$ because the corpus spans several orders of
magnitude in codebase size and bug count, making linear association inappropriate.

\paragraph{RQ3 --- Source Selection Evaluation.}
To evaluate source selection heuristics, \textbf{Kendall's $\tau$}
\citep{kendall1938new} is computed between the ordering produced by each heuristic
and the oracle ordering (sorted by actual CP-transfer MRR) for each target project
with at least three candidate sources. \textbf{Hit@1} (fraction of targets for which
the heuristic selects the oracle-best source) and the mean percentage of oracle
performance achieved by the selected source are also reported.

\subsubsection{Win/Loss Asymmetry}

A critical practical question is not just whether CPL helps on average, but

the expected benefit of adoption is 27 times larger than the expected cost of

$= +0.032$ MRR vs.\ median loss $= -0.023$ MRR) --- a symmetric risk profile where
gains and losses are comparable in magnitude. Practitioners adopting CPL with a
structure-based model face substantially higher adoption risk.

\subsubsection{Zero-Label Deployment: The Cold-Start Finding}

The most practically significant finding of RQ1 concerns the cold-start scenario.
For embedding-based models, CP-cold-start achieves MRR = 0.262 versus WP-small
MRR = 0.256 --- a difference that is not statistically significant (Wilcoxon,
$p = 0.415$, two-sided). A source-trained model applied to a new project

20\% of the target project's own data. This finding holds across all five
evaluation metrics.

(MRR = 0.027 vs.\ WP-small MRR = 0.138, Wilcoxon $p < 0.001$ in the wrong direction).

insufficient to generalise without access to any target-side signal at all.

\subsection{RQ3: How Consistent Are CPL Outcomes Across Model Implementations?}

\subsubsection{Cross-Model Direction Agreement}

For the 61 source$\to$target pairs evaluated by both embedding-based and
structure-based models, the direction of CPL gain (positive or negative) agrees
in only 25 of 61 pairs (41\%). In 18 pairs, one model benefits substantially
while the other degrades. Figure~\ref{fig:agreement_scatter} plots the per-pair
CPT$-$WPS MRR gain for both models; the scatter shows negligible correlation
between the two architectures' CPL outcomes.

This finding has a straightforward interpretation: CPL benefit is \emph{not} a
property of the source$\to$target pair alone, but is mediated by the model's ability
to generalise its learned representations across project boundaries. An architecture
that achieves high CPL benefit in 90\% of pairs and one that achieves it in 61\%
of pairs are both performing CPL, but with radically different success rates driven
by architectural differences in how cross-project representations are built and
adapted.

\subsubsection{Source Quality as a Transfer Predictor}

A natural heuristic for source project selection is to choose the source that
performs best within-project. We test this by computing Spearman $\rho$ between
the source project's WP-large MRR and the achieved CP-transfer MRR across all pairs.

For embedding-based models, $\rho = +0.01$ ($p = 0.94$): source within-project
performance has essentially zero predictive power for transfer quality. The model
learns representations that transfer independently of how well the source project
was learned to begin with. Practitioners can freely select source projects without
needing to evaluate them within-project first.

For structure-based models, $\rho = -0.316$ ($p = 0.012$): source within-project
performance is a \emph{negative} predictor of transfer quality --- sources on which
this model class performs best within-project actually transfer worse. This
counter-intuitive finding suggests that high within-project performance may reflect
source-specific feature learning that does not generalise. \emph{Practitioners
using structure-based models should not use within-project source performance as
a selection criterion.}

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Practical Implications}

The results support three concrete recommendations for practitioners considering
CPL deployment.

\paragraph{1. CPL is a low-risk, high-reward strategy for embedding-based architectures.}
If a project has few labeled bug reports and an embedding-based bug localization
tool is available, cross-project transfer with a donor project is beneficial in
90\% of deployment scenarios, with a 27:1 gain-to-loss asymmetry. Critically,
\emph{no target-project annotations are required}: zero-label deployment matches
within-project limited training, eliminating the annotation cost entirely. The
recommendation is to deploy first and annotate later, using the cold-start model
until sufficient target data is collected for fine-tuning.

\paragraph{2. Target codebase size moderates benefit; validate empirically for large targets.}
CPL gain is largest for small-codebase targets ($\Delta$MRR $\approx +0.18$ for
$<$100K LoC) and decreases for larger targets, reaching $\approx +0.10$ for
$>$300K LoC. For structure-based models, benefits essentially disappear beyond
medium-sized codebases. Practitioners with large-codebase targets should validate
CPL empirically before relying on it, particularly for structure-based tools.

\paragraph{3. Source selection has low stakes for embedding-based models.}
The most intuitive heuristic (select the source with the most bug reports) fails
to rank sources reliably (Hit@1 = 8\%), but this matters little because any
available source achieves 87\% of oracle performance. Practitioners should select
the most conveniently available source rather than investing in complex selection
procedures. For structure-based models, no reliable metadata-driven selection
rule exists, and the 24.2\% negative transfer rate means source choice has
meaningfully higher consequences.

\subsection{Implications for CPL Research}

The 41\% cross-model agreement finding is a methodological contribution for the
CPL research community. It establishes that CPL benefit cannot be assumed to
generalise across model architectures: a finding from one CPL model does not
predict outcomes from a different CPL model on the same project pairs. Future
CPL model papers should evaluate against the limited within-project baseline
(WP-small) rather than, or in addition to, within-project ample-data baselines.
Reporting results against WP-large alone creates an inflated impression of CPL
benefit by comparing against a standard that data-scarce practitioners cannot reach.

The counter-intuitive negative predictor for source quality in structure-based
models --- better within-project performance predicts worse transfer --- suggests
that within-project performance and cross-project generalisability may be
competing objectives for this architecture class. High within-project performance
may reflect overfitting to source-project-specific AST patterns that do not
transfer. This motivates CPL-specific model design that explicitly trades
within-project peak performance for cross-project robustness, an open research
direction not addressed by existing proposals.

\subsection{Threats to Validity}

\paragraph{Internal validity.}
Results for TRANP-CNN and COOBA depend on the quality of FAISS retrieval at the
first stage. We verified that retrieval recall reaches 100\% across all projects
at $K = 300$ --- every failure in the pipeline is a reranking failure, not a
retrieval failure. This rules out retrieval ceiling as a confound.

\paragraph{Construct validity.}
MRR and Top-$K$ measure file-level localisation. Finer-grained (function- or
line-level) localisation may show different transfer patterns if fine-grained
labels are available and the model architecture exploits them. The current
evaluation is aligned with the benchmarks used in prior CPL literature.

\paragraph{External validity.}
The study covers 13 Python open-source projects. Generalisability to other
programming languages, proprietary codebases, or projects with different
bug-report vocabularies and styles is empirically unvalidated. The Python scope
is by design: cross-language CPL introduces additional syntactic and semantic
variation that is a distinct research question deferred to future work.
Additionally, only open-source projects with publicly accessible commit histories
are included; proprietary codebases may have different bug-report characteristics
or code organisation patterns.

\paragraph{Conclusion validity.}
TRANP-CNN results cover only 8 pairs and are statistically underpowered. The
cross-model consistency finding is based on the 61 shared pairs evaluated by
BLAZE and COOBA. A three-model consistency test awaits completion of TRANP-CNN
evaluation across the full 63-pair set. The directional alignment between
TRANP-CNN preliminary results and BLAZE (both showing positive CPL gains in
the DS$\times$DS subset) provides tentative support that the finding will hold,
but this cannot be claimed with statistical confidence yet.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Bug localization models require labeled training data that newly started or
data-scarce projects often cannot provide. Cross-project learning has been proposed
as a solution, but no existing work has systematically compared CPL against the
practically relevant baseline of within-project limited training, nor tested whether
CPL findings hold across different model implementations.

We conducted a controlled empirical study across 63 source$\to$target project pairs,
13 open-source Python projects, and three state-of-the-art bug localization models
under four experimental scenarios designed to isolate the contribution of cross-project
knowledge.

Our answers to the three research questions are:

\textbf{RQ1.} Cross-project transfer with minimal target labels significantly and
substantially outperforms within-project limited training for embedding-based models
(win rate: 90.3\%, $d = 1.20$, all metrics $p < 0.001$). Zero-label cross-project
deployment matches within-project limited training, demonstrating that annotation
effort can be eliminated in most deployment scenarios. CPL benefit for structure-based
models is present but small ($d \approx 0.28$) and statistically marginal.

\textbf{RQ2.} CPL benefit is largest for small-codebase targets and within-domain
pairs, but neither is a prerequisite for embedding-based models. Negative transfer
is rare for embedding-based models (1.6\%) but common for structure-based models
(24.2\%). No single project-level metadata feature reliably predicts pair-level
CPL success.

\textbf{RQ3.} CPL outcomes are model-dependent: cross-model direction agreement
is only 41\% across shared pairs. Source within-project quality does not predict
transfer success for embedding-based models and is a negative predictor for
structure-based models. Embedding-based model CPL is robust to source selection
(87\% of oracle with any source), reducing the practical importance of finding
the optimal donor project.

The broader significance of these findings is that cross-project bug localization
can substitute for scarce within-project training data --- a finding that opens a
practical deployment pathway for bug localization in data-scarce projects. The
model-dependence of CPL benefit establishes that this is not a universal property
of the paradigm and motivates both architectural innovations that improve cross-project
robustness and empirical validation protocols that test CPL benefit under realistic
baseline conditions.

Future work will: (a) complete TRANP-CNN evaluation across all 63 pairs to test
three-model consistency; (b) extend the study to multi-language CPL (Java, Python
cross-language) to test whether findings generalise beyond Python; (c) develop
model-specific source selection criteria that go beyond project metadata; and
(d) evaluate CPL at function and line level for architectures that support
fine-grained localisation.
