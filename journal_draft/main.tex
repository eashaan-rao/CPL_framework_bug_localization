%% ABSTRACT
%% ===================================================================
\begin{abstract}
Whether cross-project bug localisation (CPL) succeeds appears to depend more on model
architecture than on the source-to-target project pair. This is the central finding of a
controlled multi-instrument evaluation in which three architecturally distinct bug
localisation models, embedding-based dual-encoding (BLAZE), convolutional reranking
(TRANP-CNN), and GNN-based adversarial adaptation (COOBA), are evaluated across
63 source$\to$target pairs from 13 open-source Python projects under identical bug
corpora, data splits, retrieval candidates, and baselines. Prior CPL studies evaluate a
single model against ample-data within-project baselines that data-scarce projects cannot
reach; our primary baseline is within-project limited training (WP-small, $\sim$20\% of
target labels), the data a newly active project actually has. CPL with a matched
target-label budget outperforms WP-small for 2 of 3 architectures: BLAZE with a large
effect (Cohen's $d = 1.19$, win rate 90.5\%, $p < 0.001$) and TRANP-CNN with a medium
effect ($d = 0.59$, win rate 68.3\%, $p < 0.001$), while COOBA shows marginal gains
($d = 0.27$) with a 31.7\% negative transfer rate. Two further findings sharpen the
deployment picture: a source-trained BLAZE model applied with zero target annotations is
statistically equivalent to WP-small within a 0.05 MRR margin (TOST $p = 0.007$), and
TRANP-CNN with 20\% target labels is equivalent to full within-project training within
the same margin (TOST $p < 0.001$). Cross-model direction agreement ranges from 41\% to
67\%: the same pair can help one architecture and harm another. Practitioners should
therefore validate CPL empirically for their chosen model.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Bug localisation is the task of automatically identifying the source files responsible for a reported defect.
It is a critical and labour-intensive activity in software maintenance.
Deep learning-based bug localisation models have demonstrated strong performance on
established benchmarks~\citep{lam2017bug,liang2022modeling,ciborowska2022fast,hossain2024deepdive,zhu2025funloc}.
However, their effectiveness depends on a prerequisite that is readily overlooked.
These models require substantial labelled training data: paired examples of bug reports and the
ground-truth source files modified to resolve each defect.
A project accumulates this labelled corpus only through years of sustained, tracked issue resolution.

For many projects, this accumulation either never reaches a sufficient threshold or starts from zero.
Projects at an early stage of defect tracking may have only dozens to a few hundred verified mappings
when a bug localisation tool is most needed.
The same is true of codebases recently adopting structured issue management and systems with infrequent defect reports.
The scale of this problem is illustrated by the following: the smallest project in our study has only 110 verified bug-report-to-file mappings.
A standard limited-data split yields just 22 labelled training examples from this corpus.
That is too few for reliable generalisation across a diverse codebase.
This situation is not a pathological edge case.
It is representative of any project in its first years of active bug tracking.
It is also precisely the regime where effective automated bug localisation would be most valuable.

Cross-project learning (CPL) is a natural response to this constraint.
The approach is to train a bug localisation model on a data-rich source project and then apply or fine-tune it on the data-scarce target.
The underlying intuition is that the relationship between natural-language defect descriptions and code locations
may transfer across projects that share a programming language or coding style.
Several CPL models have demonstrated the feasibility of this approach.
TRANP-CNN~\citep{huo2019deep} learns shared bug-report--code representations across projects.
COOBA~\citep{zhu2020cooba} uses adversarial domain adaptation to produce project-invariant representations.
BL-GAN~\citep{zhu2022bl} augments scarce target labels with generative models.
BLAZE~\citep{chakraborty2025blaze} extends contrastive cross-project transfer to multiple programming languages.

Despite this body of work, a fundamental question remains unanswered: does CPL effectively help the practitioners it is designed to serve?
Each of these studies demonstrates that its CPL model outperforms an ablated baseline or an earlier CPL method.
However, their within-project comparators rely on the majority of the target project's available labelled history.
Practitioners in the data-scarce regime simply cannot match this data volume.
These experiments establish that CPL surpasses a standard they cannot reach.
Whether CPL outperforms the limited within-project training they \emph{can} perform is never the comparison made.
In the analogous task of cross-project defect prediction, a systematic survey of 90 studies found that cross-project transfer is context-dependent~\citep{hosseini2017systematic}.
It helps in some conditions and hurts in others~\citep{zhang2022negative}.
No single factor reliably predicts success.
The question ``does CPL help teams that already have limited within-project data?''
is not answered anywhere in the existing bug localisation literature.

A second gap amplifies the uncertainty. Every published CPL paper evaluates a single model of its own design.
It is therefore unknown whether the demonstrated CPL benefit is a property of the cross-project paradigm
or an artefact of a particular model's inductive biases.
A practitioner who reads one CPL paper and selects a different tool cannot know whether the findings apply to their choice.
No existing study uses multiple CPL models as independent instruments across the same project pairs.

We fill both gaps with a controlled empirical study of CPL viability.
We evaluate three state-of-the-art bug localisation models spanning architecturally distinct paradigms:
embedding-based dual-encoding (BLAZE), graph-neural-network structural encoding (COOBA),
and convolutional text-code alignment (TRANP-CNN).
These models are assessed across 63 directional source$\to$target project pairs drawn from
13 open-source Python projects in four software domains (Data Science/ML, Developer Tools, Systems/Cloud, and Applications).
For each pair, we measure performance under four experimental scenarios designed to isolate the contribution of cross-project knowledge.
The primary baseline throughout is \emph{within-project limited training}: the data a newly active project actually has.
This is not the ample-data ceiling that prior work has used.

A core methodological contribution, independent of the empirical findings, is the evaluation protocol itself.
To our knowledge, no existing CPL study evaluates more than one model.
Each prior paper proposes its own architecture, selects its own project corpus, and defines its own within-project comparator.
Consequently, cross-paper comparisons conflate architectural differences with varying experimental conditions.
This fragmentation makes it impossible to attribute observed CPL outcomes to any specific cause.
In this study, all three models are evaluated on the same 63 project pairs, the same bug reports,
the same train/test splits, the same FAISS retrieval indexes, and the same primary baseline.
We treat these three architecturally distinct models as \textbf{independent scientific instruments under an identical experimental protocol}.
This uniform design allows conclusions about \emph{why} CPL succeeds or fails to be drawn with confidence.

Answering this question rigorously presents three distinct challenges.
\textbf{(C1)} Attributing CPL outcomes to model architecture requires eliminating heterogeneity
in training data, evaluation corpora, and baselines across architectures.
No prior CPL study has enforced this standard.
\textbf{(C2)} Choosing an appropriate primary baseline is conceptually subtle.
Practitioners in the data-scarce regime cannot reach the ample-data ceiling used in prior work.
Any comparison against that ceiling therefore answers the wrong question.
\textbf{(C3)} Assembling a project corpus large enough for statistical power across diverse source$\to$target pairs
requires a principled structured pair selection strategy.
A na\"{i}ve all-pairs design would exceed any realistic compute budget.

Our results answer the practitioner's question across three independent architectural instruments.
Cross-project transfer with minimal target labels outperforms within-project limited training
for 2 of 3 benchmarked paradigms: embedding-based dual-encoders show a large effect
(win rate 90.5\%, Cohen's $d = 1.19$, $p < 0.001$) and CNN-based rerankers a medium effect
(win rate 68.3\%, $d = 0.59$, $p < 0.001$), while GNN-based adversarial models show marginal
gains ($d = 0.27$) with a 31.7\% negative transfer rate. Two equivalence results extend the
picture. A source-trained embedding-based model applied with zero target annotations is
statistically equivalent to within-project limited training within a 0.05 MRR margin
(TOST $p = 0.007$). CNN-based rerankers with 20\% target fine-tuning are equivalent to
full within-project training within the same margin (TOST $p < 0.001$), the only
architecture to close the ample-data gap. Cross-model direction agreement ranges from
41\% to 67\%, and source within-project quality does not reliably predict transfer success
for any model. A practitioner deploying CPL must therefore validate the benefit
empirically for their chosen model: the same project pair can yield large gains for one
architecture and active harm for another.

The contributions of this work address these challenges:
\begin{itemize}
  \item A \textbf{controlled multi-instrument evaluation framework} assessing three
        architecturally distinct bug localisation models under an identical experimental protocol.
        All three models share the same project pairs, bug reports, data splits,
        FAISS retrieval indexes (from identical BGE embeddings), and primary baseline.
        We are not aware of any prior CPL study that provides this.
        The framework enables attribution of CPL outcomes to model architecture
        rather than experimental heterogeneity (addressing C1).
  \item The first systematic empirical comparison of CPL against within-project limited training (addressing C2).
        The study covers 63 source$\to$target pairs from 13 projects, selected via a principled
        structured strategy that maximises analytical coverage within a fixed compute budget (addressing C3).
        CPL outperforms this baseline for 2 of 3 architectural paradigms with large-to-medium effect sizes ($p < 0.001$ for both).
  \item Evidence that zero-label cross-project deployment is viable for embedding-based bug localisation.
        A source-trained model applied without any target annotations is statistically
        equivalent to within-project limited training within a 0.05 MRR margin
        (TOST $p = 0.007$) and produces practically useful rankings (MRR $\geq 0.20$)
        in 74.6\% of pairs. Additionally, CNN-based rerankers with 20\% target fine-tuning
        are statistically equivalent to full within-project training within the same margin.
        This is the highest CPL ceiling observed in this study (both findings are quantified relative to the C2 baseline).
  \item Characterisation of the conditions under which CPL is most and least effective.
        Key moderating factors include target codebase size, domain alignment, and model architecture.
        This analysis yields a three-tier negative transfer risk profile:
        3.2\% (BLAZE), 27.0\% (TRANP-CNN), and 31.7\% (COOBA), identified through the C1 uniform protocol.
  \item A cross-model consistency analysis establishing that CPL benefit is
        architecture-dependent and not a universal property of the paradigm (made possible by C1's multi-instrument design).
        Pairwise direction agreement ranges from 41\% to 67\%.
        Source within-project quality does not reliably predict transfer success for any model.
\end{itemize}

The rest of the paper is organised as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews
related work on bug localisation and CPL. Section~\ref{sec:design} describes the
study design. Section~\ref{sec:results} presents results for each research question.
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses practical implications and threats to validity.
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Bug Localisation}

Bug localisation is the task of ranking source files (or functions) by their
likelihood of containing the defect described in a natural-language bug report.
Early approaches relied on information-retrieval similarity between bug report text
and source file content \citep{zhou2012should,saha2013improving,ye2014learning,wong2016survey,wong2014brtracer,wang2014amalgam,wang2016amalgamplus}, using measures
such as BM25 or VSM over code tokens. These methods are simple and require no
training data, but their ranking quality is limited by vocabulary mismatch between
bug report prose and code identifiers.

Deep learning approaches improved on IR baselines by learning
joint representations of bug reports and code. \citet{lam2017bug} (DNNLOC)
introduced a multi-layer neural network that combines textual similarity, file
change history, and structural features. It achieved notable improvements over
IR on Java benchmarks. \citet{liang2022modeling} extended this to function-level
localisation using a graph neural network that captures call dependencies.
\citet{ciborowska2022fast} replaced conventional encoders with pre-trained BERT~\citep{devlin2019bert}
representations over code changesets. This demonstrated that transfer from large
pre-trained models reduces the labelled-data requirement. A recent survey by
\citet{niu2025deep} covers the full landscape of deep learning approaches and
confirms that labelled within-project data remains the standard assumption across
this literature. The benchmark of \citet{lee2018bench4bl} provides standardised
evaluation corpora covering 51 Java projects, and has become a reference point
for comparing bug localisation methods~\citep{bogomolov2024long}.

A consistent limitation of all within-project approaches is that they assume
the target project has accumulated sufficient labelled bug reports for training.
This assumption fails for new or data-scarce projects, which is the exact
regime that motivates the present study.

\subsection{Cross-Project Bug Localisation}

Cross-project bug localisation (CPL) addresses data scarcity by transferring
knowledge from a source project to a target. \citet{huo2019deep} proposed
TRANP-CNN, the first deep learning CPL model. It is a convolutional network that
learns shared representations of bug-report--code pairs from a source project
and applies them to a target. However, it evaluates only against an ample-data
within-project baseline, leaving the practically relevant data-scarce comparison
unanswered~\citep{zhu2021trobo}. \citet{zhu2020cooba} introduced COOBA, which uses adversarial domain
adaptation \citep{ganin2016domain} via a gradient reversal layer to learn
domain-invariant representations from AST-structured code embeddings. As we
demonstrate empirically, the adversarial objective requires target-side signal
at training time and collapses to near-zero performance without it.
\citet{zhu2022bl} proposed BL-GAN, a semi-supervised approach using generative
adversarial networks to augment scarce target labels. It does not test whether
CPL helps when the practitioner has only limited within-project data as the
available alternative. \citet{chakraborty2025blaze} proposed BLAZE, a
dual-encoder contrastive model using dynamic chunking and hard-negative mining.
It evaluates cross-project and cross-language transfer but tests only a
single architecture, leaving cross-model CPL consistency unknown~\citep{chandramohan2024supporting,li2025knowledge,reddy2025swerank}.

Despite this body of work, a critical gap remains. Each paper proposes and
evaluates its own model, typically comparing against within-project ample-data
baselines or earlier CPL methods. To our knowledge, \emph{no existing study}
(i) uses multiple CPL models as independent instruments, (ii) evaluates against
the practitioner-relevant limited within-project baseline, (iii) characterises
the conditions governing CPL success at scale, or (iv) tests cross-model
consistency of CPL findings.

A distinct emerging paradigm employs large language models in agentic interactive
workflows, such as SWE-agent~\citep{yang2024sweagent} and Agentless~\citep{xia2024agentless,zhang2024autocoderover,hossain2024deepdive}.
These systems bypass labelled-data
requirements by leveraging the model's pre-training directly for fault localisation
and patch generation. Agent-based approaches are evaluated primarily on
SWE-bench~\citep{jimenez2023swe},
which selects for well-documented issues on popular repositories with existing test suites.
The CPL setting addressed here is the complement: newly open-sourced projects,
proprietary codebases, privacy-constrained deployments, and repositories with sparse
bug reports lacking associated tests. Agent-based approaches are therefore promising for projects willing to
accept cloud-inference costs and privacy implications. The supervised CPL setting studied here remains relevant for
offline-deployable, cost-sensitive, or privacy-constrained projects.
It also applies wherever fine-grained ranking across a large codebase is required
rather than targeted patch generation. The conditions
governing CPL success identified in this study, domain alignment, embedding
quality, and inference-time adaptation mechanisms, may also inform future work on
how agents select which code to prioritise during fault localisation.

\subsection{Transfer Learning in Software Engineering}

The broader literature on cross-project transfer in software engineering provides
useful context~\citep{pan2010survey}. Cross-project defect prediction (predicting whether a software
module will be defective using training data from another project) has been
studied systematically over many years. \citet{hosseini2017systematic} survey
90 such studies and conclude that cross-project transfer is context-dependent.
It helps in some conditions and hurts in others, with no single factor reliably
predicting transfer success across all settings~\citep{sottomayor2024survey}. This finding from the defect
prediction literature motivates our characterisation of the conditions
governing CPL success in bug localisation, and our focus on negative transfer
as a deployment risk. The methodological parallel is informative: like defect
prediction, bug localisation is a within-project learning task where CPL is
possible but not guaranteed to be beneficial.

The theoretical basis for adversarial domain adaptation used in COOBA is
domain-adversarial neural networks \citep{ganin2016domain}. These minimise a
feature-level divergence between source and target distributions by training a
domain discriminator whose gradients are reversed during feature extraction.
This technique has been applied in a number of software engineering transfer
learning settings. Its effectiveness for cross-project code understanding
depends critically on whether the feature extractor captures representations that
are genuinely domain-invariant, an assumption we test empirically.

\subsection{Pre-trained Code Embeddings: Background and Design Motivation}

Recent advances in pre-trained code embeddings have raised the quality
of representations available to both within-project and cross-project models~\citep{feng2020codebert,guo2021graphcodebert,wang2021codet5,guo2022unixcoder}. This
subsection describes the embedding backbone used in our study and motivates its role.

The \texttt{BAAI/bge-code-v1} model \citep{bge_code} is a 1536-dimensional
Qwen2-based decoder pre-trained on a diverse multilingual code corpus. In this study
it powers the shared first-stage retrieval infrastructure: every bug report and source
file is embedded once with BGE, and all three models rerank the same BGE-derived FAISS
candidate pool (Section~\ref{sec:pipeline}). BLAZE additionally uses BGE as its
reranking backbone, whereas TRANP-CNN and COOBA rerank with the input representations
of their original designs (learned token embeddings and GloVe-300 with AST graphs,
respectively; Section~\ref{sec:models}). The shared retrieval stage ensures that the
candidate pool each model sees is identical, so differences in reranking behaviour are
attributable to the models themselves, of which the native input representation is a
constituent part.

Together, these four subsections of the literature frame the design choices in
Section~\ref{sec:design}. The shared BGE retrieval stage controls the candidate pool.
The three architecturally distinct models serve as
independent instruments for testing CPL consistency. The limited within-project
baseline addresses the evaluation gap identified in the CPL literature.

Figure~\ref{fig:study_design} summarises the end-to-end design.
The study is organised around a single methodological principle: to make CPL
benefit attributable to the cross-project paradigm and to model architecture,
rather than to incidental differences in training data, candidate pools,
or evaluation conditions. Every design choice documented in this section follows
from this principle and is justified explicitly below. These choices include
the shared retrieval backbone, the identical datasets and held-out test splits,
the four-scenario training framework, the choice of primary baseline, and the
structured pair selection~\citep{wohlin2012experimentation,runeson2009guidelines,feldt2010validity}.

A critical element of this controlled design is the shared retrieval infrastructure.
A \textbf{FAISS IndexFlatIP}~\citep{johnson2019billion} retrieval stage, built from identical BGE embeddings
across all target projects, serves as the first-stage candidate pool shared across
all three models.

As argued in Section~\ref{sec:intro}, prior CPL papers each propose one model with
its own corpus and comparator, conflating architecture with experimental conditions.
We depart from this practice by treating three architecturally distinct models
as \textbf{independent scientific instruments}, all evaluated under an identical
experimental protocol: the same projects, the same bug reports, the same
train/test splits, the same FAISS retrieval indexes, the same evaluation metrics,
and the same primary baseline. This controlled, multi-instrument design allows
conclusions about \emph{why} CPL succeeds or fails, not just \emph{that} it does.

\subsection{Research Questions}

This study is organised around three research questions, each targeting a distinct
aspect of CPL viability.

\begin{description}
  \item[\textbf{RQ1}] \emph{Does cross-project learning outperform within-project
    training under label scarcity?} This tests the core CPL premise against the
    practitioner-relevant baseline established in Section~\ref{sec:intro}: a project
    restricted to the limited within-project data it actually has, not the ample-data
    ceiling prior CPL work has used.

  \item[\textbf{RQ2}] \emph{What project-level characteristics determine whether CPL
    succeeds or fails?} Even if CPL is beneficial on average, practitioners need to
    know \emph{when} it is and is not appropriate. This question identifies moderating
    conditions (target size, domain alignment) and quantifies the risk of negative
    transfer.

  \item[\textbf{RQ3}] \emph{How consistent are CPL outcomes across different model
    implementations?} If CPL benefit is a property of the paradigm, outcomes should
    be consistent across architectures. If it is model-specific, practitioners
    cannot generalise findings from one tool to another. This question also
    evaluates whether project metadata reliably guides source project selection.
\end{description}

\paragraph{Hypotheses and motivated expectations.}
RQ1 is confirmatory: it tests a directional claim against a quantifiable baseline
under a controlled experimental protocol. We therefore state formal statistical
hypotheses:
\begin{description}
  \item[$H_0$:] CP-transfer does not outperform within-project limited training:
        $\mathrm{MRR}_{\mathrm{CPT}} \leq \mathrm{MRR}_{\mathrm{WPS}}$.
  \item[$H_1$:] CP-transfer outperforms within-project limited training:
        $\mathrm{MRR}_{\mathrm{CPT}} > \mathrm{MRR}_{\mathrm{WPS}}$ (one-sided).
\end{description}
This directional prediction is motivated by the body of CPL literature demonstrating
transfer feasibility~\citep{huo2019deep,chakraborty2025blaze,zhu2020cooba}, while
recognising that all prior comparisons use ample-data within-project baselines.
Whether $H_1$ holds against the limited within-project baseline that data-scarce
practitioners can actually reach is precisely what has not been tested.

RQ2 and RQ3 are exploratory rather than confirmatory, so we state
literature-grounded motivated expectations in place of formal hypotheses.
For \textbf{RQ2}, cross-project defect prediction research finds that transfer
success is context-dependent and that no single project feature reliably predicts
it~\citep{hosseini2017systematic}. We expect analogous conditions to govern CPL,
but make no a priori prediction about which moderating factors will dominate or
whether negative transfer rates will be uniform across architectures.
For \textbf{RQ3}, since we are not aware of a CPL study that evaluates more than
one model, cross-architecture consistency is empirically unknown. We expect CPL outcomes to
be at least partially architecture-mediated, given the fundamental differences in
inference-time adaptation across BLAZE, COOBA, and TRANP-CNN, but make no
prediction about the degree of divergence or whether source quality metrics will
transfer across architectures.

\subsection{Benchmarked Models}
\label{sec:models}

A central design choice is to evaluate CPL using three architecturally distinct
bug localisation models rather than a single representative. This guards against
conclusions that are artefacts of one model's inductive bias. The three models
span the dominant paradigms in the current literature.

\paragraph{BLAZE \citep{chakraborty2025blaze}.}
BLAZE is a dual-encoder contrastive model~\citep{reimers2019sentencebert} that retrieves the top-300 candidate
files via the shared FAISS index and re-ranks them by maximum cosine similarity.
Its backbone is \texttt{BAAI/bge-code-v1} \citep{bge_code}, frozen during training.
Two modal-specific residual adapter modules are trained with in-batch NTXentLoss~\citep{oord2018cpc}
($\tau = 0.3$) using hard-negative and hard-positive scaling: one for bug reports
(prefixed \texttt{search\_query:}) and one for code chunks (prefixed
\texttt{search\_document:}). At evaluation time, each source file is scored as the
maximum cosine similarity over its 512-token chunks (MaxSim aggregation). This
architecture is sensitive to the most relevant passage within a long file, which
is advantageous for large codebases.

\paragraph{COOBA \citep{zhu2020cooba}.}
COOBA is a graph neural network model that encodes source files as Abstract Syntax
Tree (AST) graphs with GloVe-300 node features, processed by graph convolutional
layers (GCNConv) with global mean pooling. Bug reports are encoded by a bidirectional
LSTM over GloVe-300 token embeddings, faithful to the original design. A shared
feature extractor over these text and AST representations produces domain-invariant
features via a Gradient Reversal Layer \citep{ganin2016domain} placed before a binary
project discriminator. The shared extractor is simultaneously optimised to minimise
ranking loss and to prevent the discriminator from distinguishing source from target
project representations. This adversarial objective ($\lambda_\mathrm{adv} = 0.1$) is
the core CPL mechanism. COOBA reranks the shared FAISS top-300 candidate pool.

\paragraph{TRANP-CNN \citep{huo2019deep}.}
TRANP-CNN is a CNN-based reranker that processes (bug report, candidate file) pairs
through parallel convolutional feature extractors (kernel sizes 3, 4, 5) and produces
scalar relevance scores. Inputs are tokenised with the BGE tokeniser vocabulary and
mapped through embedding layers trained from scratch, following the original design's
learned-representation approach. It is trained with a margin ranking loss on FAISS
top-300 candidates. TRANP-CNN represents the earliest deep learning CPL approach in
our benchmark. It provides a third architectural perspective, convolutional text-code
alignment, that complements the embedding-based and graph-structural paradigms of
BLAZE and COOBA.

Table~\ref{tab:models} summarises the three models' key properties. Together they
span the dominant implementation paradigms in the current CPL literature:
embedding-based dual-encoding (BLAZE), graph-neural-network structural encoding
(COOBA), and convolutional text-code alignment (TRANP-CNN). This diversity provides
a robust basis for testing whether CPL viability is a property of the paradigm or
of specific architectural choices.

\subsection{Dataset Selection and Curation}
\label{sec:dataset}

\paragraph{Source Benchmarks.}
Projects were drawn from two established Python bug localisation benchmarks:
\textbf{SWE-bench} \citep{jimenez2023swe} and \textbf{BeetleBox} \citep{chakraborty2025blaze}.
SWE-bench provides a curated set of popular Python repositories with verified
bug-report--to--commit-SHA ground truth, covering a broad range of application domains;
11 of the 13 final projects originate here.
BeetleBox extends Python coverage to large-scale infrastructure repositories
(ansible, localstack) that are absent from SWE-bench's scope, contributing the remaining 2.
Together, these two sources provide the Python corpora with the requisite depth of
verified bug-report ground truth needed for controlled CPL evaluation~\citep{kalliamvakou2014promises}.

\paragraph{Selection Criteria.}
A Python project from the combined SWE-bench and BeetleBox corpus was retained if
it satisfied all of the following:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep,leftmargin=*,label=(\roman*)]
  \item \emph{Bug report volume}: at least 100 unique bug reports with verified
    ground-truth file mappings (commit SHAs). Reports with missing annotations,
    ambiguous multi-file patches ($>10$ files), or non-source patches (documentation,
    CI configuration) were discarded before counting.
  \item \emph{Codebase size diversity}: the qualifying set must span at least one
    order of magnitude in lines of code, so that codebase size is a meaningful
    moderating variable for RQ2.
  \item \emph{Domain coverage}: the qualifying set must include at least one project
    per target domain class (Data Science/ML, Developer Tools, Systems/Cloud,
    Applications), so the corpus is not dominated by a single application type.
\end{enumerate}
Phase 1 restricts to Python to establish a language-homogeneous baseline before
cross-language extension in future work (Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}).

yields \textbf{13 qualifying projects}. These 13 constitute the \textbf{complete}

and none were excluded after the filtering stage. The resulting corpus spans
meaningful variation in the factors most likely to affect CPL outcomes:
codebase size ranges from 35K to 572K lines of code (one order of magnitude);

\subsection{Data Preprocessing}
\label{sec:preprocessing}

The preprocessing pipeline runs once per project and persists outputs as
project-level databases, avoiding redundant recomputation across the 756 experiment
runs.

\paragraph{Bug Report Curation.}
Raw bug reports are extracted from the two source benchmarks and cross-referenced against
repository commit history to obtain ground-truth file mappings. Each report is
uniquely identified by its commit SHA. Reports with missing SHAs, ambiguous patches
($>10$ files), or non-source patches are discarded. The resulting bug metadata
database contains \texttt{\{bug\_id, bug\_text, gt\_file\_path, commit\_sha\}}.

\paragraph{Source Code Embedding.}
All source files are extracted at the commit corresponding to each bug report
(project state at filing time). Files are encoded with \textbf{BAAI/bge-code-v1}
\citep{bge_code}, a 1536-dimensional Qwen2-based decoder pre-trained for code
understanding. Files exceeding 512 tokens are split into overlapping chunks
(\textbf{512 tokens, 50-token overlap}). Chunk embeddings are averaged to produce
a single file-level \texttt{float32} vector. Bug report texts are encoded analogously.
The pipeline uses producer-consumer multiprocessing (8 CPU workers) with batched
GPU inference (batch size 256), processing all 13 projects in approximately
6 hours on a single A100 GPU.

Per-project outputs are two binary databases. The \emph{blob embedding database}
maps each source file's blob SHA to its 1536-dimensional embedding. The
\emph{bug metadata database} maps each bug ID to its embedding and ground-truth
annotation. All three models draw their first-stage retrieval candidates from these
same databases, ensuring the candidate pool is identical across models.

\subsection{Evaluation Pipeline}
\label{sec:pipeline}

\paragraph{Retriever-Reranker Pipeline.}
All three models use the same two-stage pipeline. In the retrieval stage, a
\textbf{FAISS IndexFlatIP} index is built from L2-normalised BGE file embeddings
(inner product = cosine similarity). Each bug report is encoded, normalised, and
queried to retrieve the top-$K = 300$ candidates. Retrieval recall at $K = 300$
varies across projects (further discussed in Section~\ref{sec:threats}).
Ground-truth files not retrieved at this stage are missed at the reranking step.
In the reranking stage, each model processes the candidate set and re-sorts by
relevance score. TRANP-CNN and COOBA process (bug report, candidate file) pairs
and output relevance scores. BLAZE re-ranks the top-300 candidates by maximum
cosine similarity between the fine-tuned bug report embedding and each file's
chunk embeddings (MaxSim aggregation). Evaluation metrics are computed over the
re-sorted list. The raw FAISS ranking over file-level embeddings is itself weak
(mean MRR $= 0.029$; Table~\ref{tab:scenario_means}), largely because averaging
chunk embeddings dilutes the signal of long files: its role is candidate
\emph{recall}, while ranking precision is the rerankers' task.

\subsection{Training Scenarios}
\label{sec:scenarios}

The four training scenarios are the core methodological contribution of the study
design. They address a specific gap in how CPL has been evaluated in the existing
literature. Every prior CPL bug localisation paper evaluates its model against a
within-project baseline trained on the \emph{majority} of the target project's
available labels: the performance ceiling achievable with ample supervision.
This comparison answers the wrong question for data-scarce practitioners: they
cannot reach that ceiling. Demonstrating that a CPL model beats a standard that
no data-scarce team can achieve tells those teams nothing useful about whether CPL
will help them.

The four scenarios are designed to answer the practitioner's actual question by
forming a clean \textbf{ablation over the supervision signal available at training
time}: from none at all, to limited target data only, to limited target data
augmented with full source data, to the ample-data ceiling. The test set is held
constant throughout (20\% of target bug reports, random seed 42, identical across
all three models). Only the training data composition changes. This structure
enables pairwise comparisons that isolate the marginal value of each source of
supervision precisely.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{WP-small} (Within-Project, Limited): Model trained on approximately
    20\% of the target bug corpus, the data a newly active project actually has.
    This scenario serves as the \emph{primary baseline} for all CPL comparisons
    throughout the study. Choosing WP-small rather than WP-large as the primary
    baseline is a deliberate departure from prior CPL work. CPL is designed to help
    data-scarce projects. Measuring its benefit relative to a data-scarce baseline
    tests whether it delivers on that design intent.

  \item \textbf{WP-large} (Within-Project, Ample): Model trained on 80\% of the
    target bug corpus. It is included as the performance ceiling achievable with
    maximum within-project supervision. It is not a CPL comparator, but a reference
    showing how far CPL closes the gap toward the ideal case. The comparison
    WP-large $-$ CP-transfer quantifies what CPL still cannot achieve. The comparison
    CP-transfer $-$ WP-small quantifies what it gains over the practical baseline.

  \item \textbf{CP-cold-start} (Cross-Project, Zero-Shot): Model trained on 100\%
    of the source project's bug reports, with \emph{zero} target annotations.
    This tests zero-label deployment: can a source-trained model be applied directly
    to a new target with no annotation effort? It represents the minimum viable
    deployment scenario for a project at day zero of bug tracking. If cold-start
    performance matches WP-small, annotation cost can be eliminated entirely.

  \item \textbf{CP-transfer} (Cross-Project, Fine-Tuned): Model trained on 100\%
    of source bugs plus approximately 20\% of target bugs. The target-data budget
    is deliberately matched to WP-small. This is a critical design choice. Because
    both CP-transfer and WP-small use the same number of target-project labels,
    any performance advantage of CP-transfer over WP-small is attributable to the
    cross-project knowledge from the source project, not to additional target data.
    Without this budget-matching, the comparison would be confounded by differential
    access to target-project supervision.
\end{itemize}

This scenario framework is applied identically to all three benchmarked models.
No model-specific adjustments to scenario definitions, data splits, or evaluation
protocols are made. This uniform treatment is what makes the cross-model comparison
in RQ3 valid. Observed differences in CPL effectiveness arise from the models
themselves, not from heterogeneous experimental conditions.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}

Five complementary metrics provide a complete picture of retrieval breadth and
ranking precision, computed over the re-ranked candidate list for each test bug
report and averaged over all test reports.

\textbf{Top-$K$ Recall} ($K \in \{1, 5, 10\}$): fraction of bug reports for which
the ground-truth file appears within the top $K$ positions. Top-1 captures
precision-at-one. Top-10 captures shortlist breadth.

\textbf{Mean Average Precision (MAP)}: in the single-ground-truth setting used here,
MAP reduces to $1/r_i$ averaged over all bug reports $i$, where $r_i$ is the rank
of the correct file.

\textbf{Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR)}: mean of $1/\text{rank}$ across all bug reports.
It is identical to MAP in the single-relevant-document case. Both are reported for
comparability with prior literature.

MRR and MAP diverge from Top-$K$ when the correct file is retrieved but ranked
outside the top-$K$ shortlist. This divergence is itself informative. It reveals
whether failures are catastrophic (file not retrieved) or marginal (file ranked
too low).

\subsection{Domain Gap Measurement}
\label{sec:domaingap}

To quantify structural dissimilarity between source and target projects, a
\textbf{domain gap score} is computed for every pair by training a binary logistic
regression classifier to distinguish the two projects' file embeddings. The procedure
is as follows: (i) all file-level BGE embeddings from the source project are assigned
label 0 and from the target project label 1; (ii) a balanced 80/20 stratified split
is constructed; (iii) a \texttt{LogisticRegression} classifier with $\ell_2$
regularisation (default $C = 1.0$, up to 1000 iterations) is trained and evaluated
on the held-out split; (iv) the test accuracy is recorded as the domain gap score.

A score of 0.5 indicates that the two projects are indistinguishable in embedding
space (no domain shift). A score of 1.0 indicates fully disjoint embedding regions
(maximum domain shift). The linear probe approach has been widely used as a
separability diagnostic in the domain adaptation literature \citep{ganin2016domain}.
It avoids the risk of a more powerful classifier overfitting on small embedding
datasets. Domain gap scores for all pairs are computed once and shared across all
three model evaluations.

\subsection{Statistical Analysis Methods}
\label{sec:statmethods}

\paragraph{RQ1, Wilcoxon Signed-Rank Test.}
To assess whether CP-transfer outperforms WP-small, a \textbf{Wilcoxon signed-rank
test} \citep{wilcoxon1945,arcuri2011practical} is applied to each metric separately. This non-parametric
paired test is appropriate for two reasons. First, observations are naturally paired:
each source$\to$target pair contributes one CPT and one WPS result under identical
test conditions. Second, the distribution of improvements cannot be assumed normal
across such a diverse corpus. A one-sided alternative is tested ($H_1$: CPT $>$ WPS).
Effect sizes are reported as Cohen's $d$ \citep{cohen1988statistical,kampenes2007systematic,arcuri2014hitchhiker}
($d \geq 0.8$ large, $\geq 0.5$ medium, $\geq 0.2$ small) for comparability with
prior literature, alongside the matched-pairs rank-biserial correlation $r_{rb}$,
the effect size aligned with the signed-rank test and free of normality assumptions.

Significance markers denote unadjusted $p$-values: $\star\star\star$ ($p < 0.001$),
$\star\star$ ($p < 0.01$), $\star$ ($p < 0.05$), n.s. ($p \geq 0.05$). Where five
metrics are tested within one model family, the Bonferroni-adjusted threshold is
$\alpha/5 = 0.010$; the text states explicitly which comparisons survive this
correction.

\paragraph{Equivalence Testing (TOST).}
Claims that two conditions perform equivalently cannot rest on a non-significant
difference test, since absence of evidence is not evidence of absence. Wherever the
paper reports equivalence, we apply \textbf{two one-sided tests} (TOST)~\citep{schuirmann1987,lakens2017equivalence} implemented
as Wilcoxon signed-rank tests against null hypotheses shifted by $\pm\delta$, with an
equivalence margin of $\delta = 0.05$ MRR. This margin is approximately the smallest
mean CPL gain treated as practically meaningful in this study (TRANP-CNN's $+0.054$
MRR); differences smaller than this are of little practical consequence for adoption
decisions. Equivalence is declared when both one-sided tests reject at $\alpha = 0.05$.

\paragraph{RQ2, Spearman Rank Correlation.}
To identify project-level features predictive of CPL gain, \textbf{Spearman's $\rho$}~\citep{spearman1904}
is computed between each feature and CPT$-$WPS gain across all pairs. Spearman's
$\rho$ is preferred over Pearson's $r$ because the corpus spans several orders of
magnitude in codebase size and bug count. This makes linear association inappropriate.

\paragraph{RQ3, Source Selection Evaluation.}
To evaluate source selection heuristics, \textbf{Kendall's $\tau$}
\citep{kendall1938new} is computed between the ordering produced by each heuristic
and the oracle ordering (sorted by actual CP-transfer MRR) for each target project
with at least three candidate sources. \textbf{Hit@1} measures the fraction of
targets for which the heuristic selects the oracle-best source. The mean percentage
of oracle performance achieved by the selected source is also reported.

\paragraph{Statistical Power and Sample Size.}
With $n = 63$ paired observations, a one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test achieves
$>$99\% power to detect a medium effect ($d = 0.5$) at $\alpha = 0.05$, and
$>$80\% power to detect a small effect ($d = 0.2$). The study corpus is therefore
adequately powered for all comparisons reported in RQ1. Power was estimated
via simulation under a normal approximation to the signed-rank statistic,
using $10^{4}$ replications across a grid of effect sizes and sample sizes.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

%%,-- RQ1,--
\subsection{RQ1: Does CPL Outperform Within-Project Training Under Label Scarcity?}

\subsubsection{Scenario Performance Overview}

Cross-project transfer with minimal target labels (CP-transfer) achieves MRR = 0.392
for embedding-based models (BLAZE) versus MRR = 0.256 for within-project limited
training (WP-small), a mean absolute gain of $+0.137$ representing a 53\% relative
improvement. The scenario ordering WP-large $>$ CP-transfer $>$ CP-cold-start
$\approx$ WP-small holds across all five metrics for this model class.
This confirms that CPL provides a measurable advantage over the data the target
project already has. In practical terms, a team using CP-transfer can expect to rank the correct file
in the top~5 in roughly 53\% of queries (BLAZE CPT Top-5 = 0.527).
Without cross-project transfer, the equivalent rate is 35\%.
This is a concrete improvement for developer triage workflows.

For CNN-based rerankers (TRANP-CNN), CP-transfer achieves MRR = 0.380 versus
WP-small MRR = 0.326, a mean gain of $+0.054$ (17\% relative). Crucially, this
CP-transfer result is \emph{statistically equivalent to WP-large} (MRR = 0.389):
a TOST equivalence test bounds the difference within $\pm 0.05$ MRR ($p < 0.001$;
two-sided Wilcoxon $p = 0.37$, 49.2\% win rate). TRANP-CNN is the only model
in this study for which cross-project transfer with 20\% target fine-tuning reaches
the performance achievable with full within-project training.

For GNN-based adversarial models (COOBA), the corresponding gain is $+0.018$ MRR
(13\% relative), smaller in both absolute and relative terms, and
CP-cold-start collapses to near-zero (MRR = 0.027), indicating that zero-shot
transfer fails without any target-side signal for this architecture class.

\subsubsection{Statistical Significance and Effect Size}

Table~\ref{tab:wilcoxon} presents Wilcoxon signed-rank test results and Cohen's $d$
for the CP-transfer versus WP-small comparison across all metrics and models.

For embedding-based models (BLAZE), cross-project transfer significantly outperforms
within-project limited training across all five metrics ($p < 0.001$, $\star\star\star$
on all metrics). Win rates range from 82.5\% (Top-1) to 93.7\% (MAP). Effect sizes
are large by Cohen's convention on all metrics ($d = 1.04$--$1.19$; rank-biserial
$r_{rb} = 0.95$--$0.98$), indicating that
the CPL advantage is not only statistically reliable but practically substantial.
A large effect ($d > 1$) means the CPL gain is detectable even in studies with as
few as 10 project pairs: the benefit is unmistakable in scale.

For CNN-based rerankers (TRANP-CNN), cross-project transfer also significantly
outperforms within-project limited training across all five metrics. Win rates range
from 54.0\% (Top-1) to 71.4\% (MAP). Effect sizes are medium ($d = 0.32$--$0.59$;
$r_{rb} = 0.37$--$0.58$),
with $p < 0.001$ on MRR, MAP, and Top-5; $p < 0.01$ on Top-1; and $p < 0.05$ on
Top-10, the only TRANP-CNN metric not surviving the Bonferroni-adjusted threshold
of $0.010$. These results establish TRANP-CNN as the second model, alongside BLAZE,
for which CPL is statistically significant and practically meaningful across the full
metric set. A medium effect means the advantage is real and reproducible, though
a larger sample would be needed to detect it reliably in a smaller study.

For GNN-based adversarial models (COOBA), the CPL advantage is present but marginal:
nominally significant on MRR, MAP, Top-1, and Top-10 ($p = 0.012$--$0.046$, $\star$)
and borderline on Top-5 ($p = 0.052$). None of the five metrics survives the
Bonferroni-adjusted threshold of $0.010$. Win rates range from 43.5\% (Top-1) to
60.3\% (MRR/MAP), close to chance on several metrics. Effect sizes are small
($d = 0.20$--$0.31$; $r_{rb} = 0.24$--$0.39$), statistically detectable at 63 pairs
but too small to provide consistent benefit across individual deployments.

We classify an architectural paradigm as exhibiting \emph{practically beneficial}
CPL if it meets both criteria: statistical significance on the primary metrics
(MRR and MAP) at the Bonferroni-adjusted threshold ($p < 0.010$)
\emph{and} at least a medium effect size ($d \geq 0.5$). The medium-effect threshold
is the conventional minimum for consistent individual-deployment benefit. A small
effect is statistically detectable at $n = 63$ but too small to reliably manifest
in any single project pair. Under this criterion, BLAZE ($d = 1.19$) and TRANP-CNN
($d = 0.59$) meet both criteria. COOBA ($d = 0.27$; no metric surviving correction)
meets neither.

Taken together, \textbf{2 of 3 benchmarked architectural paradigms show statistically
significant CPL benefit with a medium-to-large effect size.} This cross-architecture
replication is the primary evidence that CPL is a viable strategy for data-scarce
projects, rather than an artefact of any particular model's inductive bias.
The three-tier effect size profile ($d \approx 1.19$ for BLAZE, $0.59$ for
TRANP-CNN, $0.27$ for COOBA) maps to deployment risk. BLAZE and
TRANP-CNN are architectures where CPL can be recommended with confidence.
COOBA requires empirical validation before adoption.

\subsubsection{Win/Loss Asymmetry}

A critical practical question is not just whether CPL helps on average, but
\emph{what happens when it does not help}. Asymmetry between gains and losses is
practically more important than win rate alone. A model that wins 60\% of the time
but loses catastrophically in the other 40\% is worse than one that wins 80\% with
smaller, negligible losses. The CPL gain distribution for embedding-based models
(BLAZE) shows a pronounced asymmetry. When CP-transfer outperforms WP-small it
does so (median win $= +0.134$ MRR), but when it does not, the loss
is negligible (median loss $= -0.005$ MRR). The asymmetry ratio, median gain
divided by median loss magnitude, is $27\times$ for MRR. BLAZE's expected benefit
of adoption is 27 times larger than the expected cost of failure, making it a
low-risk default for CPL deployment.

For CNN-based rerankers (TRANP-CNN), the asymmetry ratio is $2.8\times$ (median
win $= +0.068$ MRR vs.\ median loss $= -0.025$ MRR). This is a moderate asymmetry
that still favours adoption, but with meaningfully higher loss exposure than
embedding-based models. Practitioners using TRANP-CNN should expect that roughly one in three
deployments will underperform limited within-project training, though the gains
in the remaining deployments are larger.

For GNN-based adversarial models (COOBA), the asymmetry ratio falls to only
$1.5\times$ (median win $= +0.032$ MRR vs.\ median loss $= -0.022$ MRR), a
near-symmetric risk profile where gains and losses are comparable in magnitude.
Practitioners adopting COOBA for CPL should expect losses comparable in magnitude to their gains in nearly
a third of deployments. This risk profile calls for empirical validation before
committing to the strategy.
The three-way asymmetry spectrum ($27\times \to 2.8\times \to 1.5\times$) mirrors
the effect-size ordering and provides a practical risk ranking for CPL adoption
decisions. Figure~\ref{fig:gain_distribution} illustrates the gain distributions.

\subsubsection{Zero-Label Deployment: The Cold-Start Finding}

Cold-start CPL (applying a source-trained model with zero target annotations)
represents the most practically valuable scenario for data-scarce teams. It removes
the annotation bottleneck entirely. Testing whether it is viable is therefore
central to the utility case for CPL.

For embedding-based models, CP-cold-start achieves MRR = 0.262 versus WP-small
MRR = 0.256. No difference is detectable on any of the five metrics (two-sided
Wilcoxon, all $p \geq 0.58$), and a TOST equivalence test establishes equivalence
within a $\pm 0.05$ MRR margin ($p = 0.007$). A source-trained model applied to a
new project \emph{without any target annotations} is therefore statistically
equivalent, up to a practically negligible margin, to training on 20\% of the
target project's own data.

Figure~\ref{fig:coldstart_heatmap} shows the cold-start viability heatmap across
all source$\to$target combinations. In 74.6\% of pairs (47/63), the cold-start
model achieves an MRR of at least 0.20, which corresponds to the ground-truth file
appearing around rank five on average, the shortlist depth a developer can
reasonably inspect during triage. It suggests that annotation effort can be eliminated
entirely for the majority of deployment scenarios when an appropriate source project
is available. For a newly open-sourced project with no labelled bugs at all, a BLAZE
model trained on any within-domain source project can be deployed immediately at
near within-project performance, with zero annotation cost.

GNN-based adversarial models (COOBA) collapse to near-zero performance in cold-start
(MRR = 0.027), essentially identical to the raw FAISS retrieval baseline
(MRR = 0.029) and far below WP-small (MRR = 0.138, $p < 0.001$). CNN-based rerankers
(TRANP-CNN) also fail cold-start, though to a lesser degree: MRR = 0.075 versus
WP-small MRR = 0.326 ($p < 0.001$). TRANP-CNN cold-start is 2.6$\times$ the FAISS
baseline, indicating some residual utility from source-trained convolution weights.
It still represents only 23\% of within-project limited training performance. Both
scalar-ranking architectures require target-side calibration. Without target examples,
their scoring functions remain anchored to source-project distributions and do not
transfer to the target's ranking difficulty. The cold-start dichotomy is architectural:
BLAZE's embedding similarity generalises directly to unseen targets, while TRANP-CNN
and COOBA's learned ranking scores do not.

\subsection{RQ2: What Project-Level Characteristics Determine CPL Success or Failure?}

\subsubsection{Target Codebase Size}

Target codebase size is the strongest observable moderator of CPL benefit
in this study. Knowing a project's LoC category gives a practitioner an indicative
upper bound on expected gain. Table~\ref{tab:stratified} presents CPL gain stratified
by target codebase size.

For embedding-based models (BLAZE), CPL gain is largest for small-codebase targets
(mean $\Delta$MRR $= +0.179$ for $<$100K LoC) but remains meaningful for all size
groups ($+0.110$ for medium, $+0.106$ for large). Cross-project transfer remains positive
across all target size groups in this corpus, making BLAZE a viable choice even for large, complex codebases.

For CNN-based rerankers (TRANP-CNN), the LoC pattern reveals a notable finding.
CP-transfer \emph{exceeds} WP-large for small targets (CPT = 0.532 vs.\
WPL = 0.518), the only size-group aggregate in this study where cross-project
transfer surpasses the within-project performance ceiling (individual pairs also
cross this line; see Section~\ref{sec:cases}). The cross-project initialisation
teaches the model what bug-localisation signal looks like. The 20\% target
fine-tuning then calibrates it, together outperforming training from scratch on
four times more local data. Medium and large targets show modest gains ($+0.024$
and $+0.062$ respectively).

For GNN-based adversarial models (COOBA), target LoC acts as a near-hard ceiling.
Medium targets gain $+0.018$ MRR and large targets gain only $+0.006$ MRR,
essentially no transfer benefit. CPL is only worth attempting for COOBA on small
targets ($+0.025$ MRR). For medium and large targets the gain is smaller than
measurement noise. GNN-based adversarial models fail to generalise their
graph-structured representations to large, architecturally complex codebases even
with source-side pre-training.

These size-stratified gains should be interpreted cautiously: FAISS recall at $K = 300$
falls below 50\% for the three projects with the largest codebases (ansible 31.3\%,
wagtail 34.6\%, meson 45.5\%). Part of the apparent LoC-driven degradation therefore
reflects a shared retrieval ceiling rather than model architectural limitation alone.
The within-model CPL comparison (CPT vs.\ WPS) is unaffected because both conditions
face the same retrieval constraint, but the absolute gain magnitudes for large targets
should be read as suggestive rather than definitive.

\subsubsection{Negative Transfer}

Negative transfer (where source-project knowledge actively harms target performance)
is the central risk in CPL. Understanding when it occurs determines whether CPL
is a safe default or a strategy requiring careful source selection.

We define \emph{negative transfer} as CP-transfer MRR $<$ WP-small MRR by more
than 0.01 points. The neutral band $|\Delta| \leq 0.01$ is treated as no-effect.

For embedding-based models (BLAZE), only 2 of 63 pairs (3.2\%) exhibit negative
transfer under this definition, essentially zero adoption risk. The two cases are
prefecthq/prefect $\to$ ansible/ansible ($-$0.023 MRR) and jupyterlab/jupyterlab
$\to$ localstack/localstack ($-$0.016 MRR). Both are cross-domain pairs with large
targets, and both are negligible in absolute magnitude.

For CNN-based rerankers (TRANP-CNN), negative transfer affects 17 of 63 pairs
(27.0\%), roughly one in three pairs. The worst cases include ansible $\to$ prefect
($-$0.094 MRR), jupyterlab $\to$ meson ($-$0.087 MRR), and lightning $\to$ ansible
($-$0.078 MRR). These cases concentrate in cross-domain settings where
high-specificity codebases (ansible, prefect, meson) appear as targets.

For GNN-based adversarial models (COOBA), negative transfer affects 20 of 63 pairs
(31.7\%), nearly a third of all pairs. The worst cases include meson $\to$ jupyterlab
($-$0.152 MRR), qiskit $\to$ jupyterlab ($-$0.084 MRR), and ansible $\to$ jupyterlab
($-$0.081 MRR). The consistent appearance of jupyterlab as the most-harmed target
suggests that COOBA's AST-based representations overfit to source-project structural
patterns that conflict with jupyterlab's frontend-heavy codebase.

A 27--32\% negative transfer rate in TRANP-CNN and COOBA means that roughly one in
three CPL deployments will see the model perform worse than limited target training
alone. Practitioners should treat TRANP-CNN and COOBA CPL as a strategy requiring
empirical validation per deployment, not a universally safe upgrade.

The three-model spectrum (3.2\%$\to$27.0\%$\to$31.7\%) aligns with the win/loss
asymmetry ordering and provides a consistent risk profile for each architectural
paradigm.

Figure~\ref{fig:feature_corr} plots Spearman correlations between project-level
metadata features and CPL gain. A key finding is that no single measured feature
reliably predicts CPL success or failure at the pair level for any model class.
None of LoC ratio ($\rho < 0.15$), domain gap accuracy ($|\rho| < 0.12$), source
bug count ($|\rho| < 0.08$), or bug report verbosity provides a reliable predictor
when considered in isolation. This does not contradict the size-stratified finding
above: target LoC stratifies \emph{mean} gain into coarse categorical bands, whereas
no continuous feature, including the source-to-target LoC ratio, ranks
\emph{individual pairs} reliably. Negative transfer is not predictable from project
metadata alone. Empirical validation is the safest guard against deployment risk.

\subsubsection{Source-Target Domain Alignment}

Table~\ref{tab:crossdomain} presents CPL gain broken down by whether source and
target belong to the same domain (within-domain, Group A pairs) or different domains
(cross-domain, Groups B and C); Figure~\ref{fig:cross_domain} shows the
corresponding gain distributions.

For embedding-based models (BLAZE), within-domain pairs gain more on average
($+0.180$ MRR, 93.3\% win rate) than cross-domain pairs ($+0.097$ MRR,
78.8\% win rate), a 14.5 percentage-point drop across domain boundaries.
This is a notable degradation, but cross-domain BLAZE CPL remains strong
in absolute terms (78.8\% win rate), and negative transfer in cross-domain
pairs remains rare. \emph{Domain alignment amplifies CPL benefit for BLAZE
but is not a requirement for it.} Practitioners can attempt cross-domain
CPL with BLAZE but should expect modestly lower win rates than within-domain.

For CNN-based rerankers (TRANP-CNN), domain alignment has a pronounced moderating
effect: within-domain win rate = 76.7\%, cross-domain win rate = 48.5\%, a
28 percentage-point drop that is the steepest of the three models. For TRANP-CNN,
cross-domain pairs fall below the 50\% win rate threshold. CPL is more likely to
hurt than help when source and target belong to different domains. This steep
cross-domain sensitivity confirms that domain gap is the primary risk factor for
CNN-based CPL. Practitioners should prioritise within-domain sources when using
TRANP-CNN-style models.

For GNN-based adversarial models (COOBA), domain alignment has a negligible effect: within-domain win rate = 43.3\%, cross-domain win
rate = 45.5\%, a difference of only $-$2 percentage points (cross-domain
slightly higher). COOBA's CPL outcome is essentially independent of domain
proximity. This finding contradicts the naive expectation that adversarial
domain adaptation would be most effective within-domain. Instead, COOBA's
structural (AST-based) overfitting to the source project determines success or
failure regardless of domain, making domain alignment an unreliable guide for
COOBA source selection.

The domain gap metric (logistic regression accuracy on file embeddings,
Section~\ref{sec:domaingap}) shows no significant correlation with CPL gain
for any model. For BLAZE and COOBA, $|\rho| < 0.12$ (all $p > 0.20$).
For TRANP-CNN, $\rho = 0.11$ ($p = 0.39$). Continuous embedding-space distance does not reliably
capture the domain alignment that governs TRANP-CNN outcomes. The domain binary
(same vs.\ different application category) is a stronger signal than the continuous
domain gap metric for TRANP-CNN, but a non-signal for COOBA.

\subsubsection{Commutativity: Does Transfer Direction Matter?}

For 28 symmetric pairs (both $A \to B$ and $B \to A$ evaluated), we test whether
the smaller-LoC target consistently gains more from CPL. This hypothesis is motivated
by the LoC-stratified results: if smaller codebases benefit more on average, the
same asymmetry should appear within symmetric pairs.

For embedding-based models (BLAZE), 67.9\% of symmetric pairs (19/28) show the
smaller-LoC target gaining more, above the 50\% random baseline and consistent with
the hypothesis that data-scarce targets gain most from external knowledge. For
CNN-based rerankers (TRANP-CNN), the consistency rate is 50.0\% (14/28),
essentially random. LoC asymmetry does not predict transfer direction for TRANP-CNN.
Domain alignment and bug distribution are the stronger factors.
For GNN-based adversarial models (COOBA), the consistency rate is 60.7\% (17/28),
a moderate tendency that aligns with the LoC-stratified results showing stronger
CPL gains for small targets. This stands in contrast to TRANP-CNN's random
commutativity (50.0\%), confirming that COOBA's failure mode is LoC-driven even
if its success mode is not domain-driven.

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=gray!10, colframe=gray!50, title=\textbf{RQ2 Summary}]
CPL benefit is largest for small-codebase targets and within-domain pairs, but
neither is a prerequisite for embedding-based models. The three models exhibit a
consistent risk spectrum:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep,topsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{BLAZE:} 3.2\% negative transfer, 14.5pp cross-domain sensitivity
        (moderate), cross-domain CPL still viable at 78.8\% win rate.
  \item \textbf{TRANP-CNN:} 27.0\% negative transfer, 28pp cross-domain sensitivity
        (steepest of the three), CPT exceeds WPL for small targets.
  \item \textbf{COOBA:} 31.7\% negative transfer, LoC-sensitive (gains vanish for
        large targets), no domain sensitivity ($-$2pp, within- and cross-domain
        outcomes are equivalent).
\end{itemize}
No single project-level metadata feature reliably predicts pair-level CPL success
for any model.
\end{tcolorbox}

\subsubsection{Cross-Model Direction Agreement}

the pair (e.g., compatible code styles, domain overlap), all models should agree.
If CPL is architecture-mediated, they should diverge. With all three models fully
evaluated on the same 63 source$\to$target pairs, we report pairwise direction
agreement across all three model combinations.

The pairwise results reveal a structured pattern. BLAZE and TRANP-CNN agree in 67\%

either shares with COOBA. COOBA agrees with BLAZE in only 41\% of pairs and

pronounced divergences, BLAZE gains $+0.374$ MRR on the ansible~$\to$~jupyterlab pair

Figure~\ref{fig:agreement_scatter} shows the cross-model scatter plots.

This pattern has a natural interpretation. BLAZE and TRANP-CNN are both
\emph{learned-ranker} models that assign relevance scores to candidates at inference
time. They differ in mechanism (cosine similarity versus CNN scalar) but share the
property that cross-project knowledge is injected at training time and applied at
ranking time to the target's files. COOBA's adversarial domain adaptation mechanism
trains a discriminator that erases source-target distinctions from the feature space.
This is a fundamentally different adaptation strategy that produces different CPL
sensitivity to the same project pairs.

CPL benefit is \emph{not} a property of the source$\to$target pair alone. It is
mediated by the model's adaptation mechanism. A practitioner cannot infer from one
model's CPL outcome what a different architecture will do on the same pair.
The agreement range across all three model combinations (41\% to 67\%) confirms
that architecture appears to be a stronger determinant of CPL outcomes than the project pair.

\subsubsection{Source Quality as a Transfer Predictor}

A natural heuristic for source project selection is to choose the source that
performs best within-project. We test this by computing Spearman $\rho$ between
the source project's WP-large MRR and the achieved CP-transfer MRR across all pairs.

For embedding-based models (BLAZE), $\rho = -0.00$ ($p = 0.999$): source within-project
performance has trivially zero predictive power for transfer quality. Practitioners
can freely select source projects without evaluating them within-project first.

For CNN-based rerankers (TRANP-CNN), $\rho = -0.208$ ($p = 0.102$): the direction is
negative but does not reach significance. The practical conclusion mirrors BLAZE:
source within-project performance is not a useful selection criterion.

For GNN-based adversarial models (COOBA), $\rho = -0.296$ ($p = 0.019$): source
within-project performance is a statistically significant \emph{negative} predictor
of transfer quality. Sources on which COOBA performs best within-project actually
transfer worse. This counter-intuitive finding arises because COOBA overfits to the
structural patterns of the source when it performs well within-project, making those
patterns a liability rather than an asset when applied to the target.
\emph{Practitioners using COOBA should not use within-project source performance as a selection criterion. It is actively misleading.}

All three models thus agree on the practical recommendation: source selection
based on within-project quality metrics is unreliable or counter-productive across
all three architectural paradigms.

\subsubsection{Source Selection Heuristics}

Table~\ref{tab:source_selection} summarises the evaluation of two source selection
heuristics against the oracle upper bound, across all targets with at least three
candidate sources. The heuristics are: \emph{most-bugs} (select the source with the
largest bug corpus) and \emph{domain-gap} (select the source with the lowest
embedding-space domain gap, i.e.\ the most similar project). Hit@1 measures how
often the heuristic selects the oracle-best source. Kendall $\tau$ measures the
quality of the full source ranking. \% oracle measures CP-transfer MRR achieved by
the selected source relative to the best possible source.

For embedding-based models (BLAZE), the most-bugs heuristic achieves Hit@1 = 15.4\%
and $\tau = -0.055$ (near-zero, weakly negative). The domain-gap heuristic does
better on Hit@1 (46.2\%) but shows a similar negative $\tau$ ($-0.192$). Neither
heuristic reliably ranks the full source list. Despite this, BLAZE achieves
88.6\%--92.7\% of oracle performance with either heuristic. Source selection has low stakes for BLAZE. Even a poorly chosen source produces near-oracle results.

For CNN-based rerankers (TRANP-CNN), most-bugs achieves Hit@1 = 30.8\% with
$\tau = +0.110$, the only positive $\tau$ of any heuristic across all three models.
Domain-gap achieves Hit@1 = 23.1\% with $\tau = -0.080$. Oracle recovery is
86.7\%--89.8\% for both heuristics. The 27.0\% negative transfer risk means that
within-domain validation is advisable regardless of which heuristic is used.

For GNN-based adversarial models (COOBA), most-bugs achieves Hit@1 = 38.5\% with
$\tau = -0.194$. Domain-gap achieves Hit@1 = 23.1\% with $\tau \approx 0$
($-0.007$). Both heuristics achieve 78.8\%--79.2\% of oracle performance, the
lowest oracle recovery of the three models. The near-zero $\tau$ for domain-gap is
particularly striking. Domain similarity provides essentially no signal for ranking
sources when the CPL mechanism is adversarial domain adaptation, which already
attempts to erase domain-specific features from the learned representations.

The practical takeaway is consistent across both heuristics and all three models
(Figure~\ref{fig:ss_quality} visualises ranking quality under the most-bugs
heuristic). No tested heuristic reliably ranks sources ($\tau < 0.2$ in all cases),
yet all three models achieve 79--93\% of oracle performance with any heuristic. Practitioners
do not need sophisticated source selection for BLAZE or TRANP-CNN. For COOBA, source
selection matters more (lower absolute oracle recovery), but neither heuristic is
reliable. Empirical holdout validation on a small target sample remains the safest
strategy.

\subsection{Cross-Model Synthesis: What the Evidence Says About CPL}
Across all three model architectures and 63 project pairs, the empirical evidence
points to a consistent conclusion. \textbf{Cross-project learning is a viable
strategy for data-scarce bug localisation in 2 of 3 architectural paradigms tested,
but its effectiveness and risk profile are architecture-dependent.}

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep,topsep=2pt]
  \item \textit{\textbf{CPL delivers meaningful benefit in 2 of 3 models tested}}
        (BLAZE $d = 1.19$, TRANP-CNN $d = 0.59$, across all five metrics); COOBA's
        marginal gains ($d = 0.27$) border on practical irrelevance for individual
        deployments.
  \item \textit{\textbf{Cold-start deployment is viable for embedding-based models
        only}}; the two scalar-ranking models require target-side calibration to
        produce useful rankings.
  \item \textit{\textbf{With minimal target fine-tuning, CPL can match or exceed full
        within-project training}}: TRANP-CNN CP-transfer is equivalent to WP-large
        (TOST, $\pm 0.05$ MRR), and exceeds it for small-codebase targets.
  \item \textit{\textbf{Negative transfer is a material risk for CNN and GNN models}}
        (27.0\% and 31.7\% of pairs respectively, versus 3.2\% for BLAZE):
        cross-domain-driven for TRANP-CNN, LoC-driven and domain-agnostic for COOBA.
  \item \textit{\textbf{CPL outcome is not predictable from the project pair alone.}}
        Cross-model agreement of 41--67\% means the same pair can benefit one
        architecture and harm another; a CPL result does not generalise from one
        tool to another without empirical validation.
\end{itemize}

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Practical Implications}

The results support five concrete recommendations for practitioners considering
CPL deployment.

\paragraph{1. CPL is a low-risk, high-reward strategy for embedding-based architectures.}
If a project has few labelled bug reports and an embedding-based bug localisation
tool is available, cross-project transfer is beneficial in 90\% of deployment
scenarios, with a 27:1 gain-to-loss asymmetry. Critically,
\emph{no target-project annotations are required}: zero-label deployment is
statistically equivalent to within-project limited training (TOST, $\pm 0.05$ MRR),
removing the annotation cost. The
recommended strategy is immediate deployment, with annotations accumulated subsequently
as target data becomes available for fine-tuning.

\paragraph{2. Target codebase size moderates benefit; validate empirically for large targets.}
CPL gain is largest for small-codebase targets ($\Delta$MRR $\approx +0.179$ for
$<$100K LoC) and decreases for larger targets, reaching $\approx +0.106$ for
$>$300K LoC. For GNN-based adversarial models (COOBA), benefits essentially disappear
beyond medium-sized codebases ($+0.006$ MRR for large targets). Practitioners with
large-codebase targets and COOBA-style tools should validate CPL empirically before
relying on it.

\paragraph{3. Choose sources by domain, not by ranking heuristics or within-project quality.}
No tested heuristic reliably ranks source projects for any model ($|\tau| < 0.2$ and
Hit@1 of 15.4--38.5\% across all model-heuristic combinations), yet all three models
achieve 79--93\% of oracle CP-transfer performance regardless of source choice, so
source selection has low stakes for BLAZE and TRANP-CNN. The most actionable
criterion is domain category membership: same-domain sources produce a 28
percentage-point higher win rate for CNN-based rerankers, and selecting any
conveniently available \emph{within-domain} source is preferable to investing in
source ranking by bug corpus size or embedding-space proximity. Source
within-project quality should not be used as a selection criterion for any
architecture; for GNN-based adversarial models it is actively misleading, since
higher source within-project MRR is a statistically significant \emph{negative}
predictor of transfer quality ($\rho = -0.296$, $p = 0.019$), reflecting
overfitting to source-specific AST patterns. For COOBA, where oracle recovery is
lowest (78.8\%) and negative transfer is highest (31.7\%), empirical holdout
validation on a small target sample is advisable.

\paragraph{4. For the highest CPL ceiling, CNN-based rerankers with 20\% target labels reach full within-project training performance.}
When a project has at least 20\% of its bug reports labelled and domain-aligned sources
are available, TRANP-CNN achieves CP-transfer performance statistically equivalent
to WP-large (TOST, $\pm 0.05$ MRR). For small-codebase targets it actually exceeds WP-large.
This provides the highest absolute CPL performance of the three models, but carries
27.0\% negative transfer risk concentrated in cross-domain pairs. Practitioners
should validate domain alignment before deploying CNN-based CPL cross-domain.

\paragraph{5. Empirical holdout validation is the safest guard against negative transfer.}
No project-level metadata feature (LoC ratio, domain gap score, source bug count,
or bug report verbosity) reliably predicts pair-level CPL gain for any model
($|\rho| < 0.15$ for all feature-model combinations). When deployment risk is high,
the safest approach is to validate CPL empirically on a small annotated holdout from
the target project before committing to cross-project transfer as the primary
localisation strategy. This is especially important for TRANP-CNN and COOBA, where
roughly one in three deployments yields negative transfer.

\subsection{Architectural Interpretation of CPL Profiles}
\label{sec:architecture}

The three models in this study span three distinct paradigms of how a bug
localisation model evaluates candidate files at inference time. The experimental
design was structured to make architectural attribution possible rather than merely
correlational. All three models are evaluated on identical datasets with identical
train/test splits (random seed~42), retrieve candidates from the same BGE-based
FAISS indexes, and are benchmarked across the same 63 project pairs. Training data
access, retrieval candidates, and pair selection are held constant across the three
model evaluations. Each model consumes its native input representation (BGE
embeddings for BLAZE, learned token embeddings for TRANP-CNN, GloVe-300 text and
AST features for COOBA), which we treat as a constituent of the architecture under
test. The variable distinguishing the three CPL profiles is therefore each model's
\textbf{evaluation mechanism}: what its learned parameters and representations do to
rank candidates at test time. This controlled design is what allows the performance
differences to be attributed to architectural choices rather than to confounding
experimental conditions.

The divergent CPL profiles map cleanly onto that mechanism.

\paragraph{BLAZE (embedding-based dual-encoder).}
BLAZE scores candidate files at inference time using cosine similarity between
frozen BGE backbone embeddings. No learned scalar weight is applied at ranking time.
The ordering is determined entirely by pre-trained representations built on a diverse
multilingual code corpus that already carry cross-project semantics before any
fine-tuning begins. Cold-start viability follows from this mechanism:
there is nothing project-specific to calibrate, because no target-specific ranking
weights exist. The 27:1 gain-to-loss asymmetry follows from the same property.
Similarity-based ranking is inherently smooth. A suboptimal source project produces
suboptimal but not catastrophically wrong cosine similarities, bounding loss
magnitude. Gains from well-aligned source-target embedding spaces can be large.
Source selection therefore has low stakes: even a poorly chosen source project
produces rankings that are only marginally off.

Two competing hypotheses warrant consideration. \textit{(i) Representation quality}:
BLAZE reranks with modern, frozen BGE embeddings (adapter-only fine-tuning), while
TRANP-CNN and COOBA rerank with the representations of their original designs
(learned token embeddings and GloVe-300, respectively). One might ask whether
BLAZE's superior CPL profile is driven by representation quality rather than its
evaluation mechanism. \textit{(ii) Model age}: BLAZE (2025) is four to six years
newer than COOBA (2021) and TRANP-CNN (2019). One might ask whether the performance
gap reflects general progress rather than architectural differences.
Three observations make the evaluation-mechanism account more parsimonious, though
the explanations are not mutually exclusive. First, if representation quality were
the sole factor, cold-start performance should be near-optimal even without adapter
training. However, CP-cold-start achieves only MRR = 0.262 versus CP-transfer
MRR = 0.392, indicating that adapter fine-tuning on source data still provides
meaningful signal beyond the pre-trained representations. Second, the 27:1
gain-to-loss \emph{asymmetry}, the ratio of expected gain to expected loss when CPL
does not help, is a property of ranking smoothness, not of representation capacity.
A strong backbone can produce arbitrarily bad rankings if source and target
embedding distributions are misaligned, but cosine similarity produces smooth,
bounded losses by virtue of its range. Third, the two non-BGE rerankers differ
from each other in CPL profile ($d = 0.59$ vs.\ $0.27$), so
representation vintage alone does not determine transfer behaviour. Section~\ref{sec:threats}
records the residual representation asymmetry as a construct threat.

\paragraph{COOBA (GNN with adversarial domain adaptation).}
COOBA trains a Gradient Reversal Layer discriminator to erase project-specific
signals from the shared GNN feature space. This adversarial objective is the CPL
mechanism. By preventing the discriminator from distinguishing source from target
representations, the encoder is forced to produce domain-invariant features.
However, the mechanism requires target-side representations \emph{during training}
so the discriminator has something to contrast against the source distribution.
With zero target data, the discriminator receives no target signal. The resulting
features remain calibrated to the source AST vocabulary. AST vocabularies, including
identifier namespaces, call-graph structures, and type hierarchies, are
project-specific. The features therefore misalign when applied to a new target,
explaining the near-zero cold-start MRR ($0.027$ vs.\ $0.138$ for WP-small). The
counter-intuitive finding that higher source within-project performance predicts
\emph{worse} transfer follows from the same logic. A model that has learned source
AST patterns very precisely has encoded more source-specific features, which
generalise less to a new project's AST vocabulary.

\paragraph{TRANP-CNN (CNN reranker).}
TRANP-CNN produces a scalar relevance score for each (bug report, candidate file)
pair via parallel convolutional feature extractors and a margin ranking loss.
This scalar is calibrated during training to the ranking difficulty of the source
project's file distribution. Without target examples, the score function remains
anchored to the source distribution. Its scale and decision boundary do not transfer
to the target, explaining cold-start failure (MRR = 0.075 vs.\ WP-small 0.326).
With 20\% target fine-tuning (CP-transfer), TRANP-CNN benefits from source
pre-training and recalibrates to the target's ranking difficulty. This yields a
medium-effect CPL profile ($d = 0.59$, $p < 0.001$ across all metrics), intermediate between
COOBA ($d = 0.27$) and BLAZE ($d = 1.19$). Notably, this recalibrated CP-transfer
result is statistically equivalent to WP-large (TOST, $\pm 0.05$ MRR). Source
pre-training provides a quality of initialisation that closes the gap to the
ample-data ceiling once target calibration is provided.

A particularly important cross-check on the architectural interpretation is the
chronological ordering of the three models. COOBA (2021) employs a more
sophisticated adversarial adaptation mechanism than TRANP-CNN (2019), yet achieves
worse CPL outcomes across nearly all metrics. If model age or
methodological sophistication were the explanatory variable, COOBA should outperform
TRANP-CNN. It does not. This rules out publication year and architectural complexity
as confounds, leaving the evaluation mechanism as the consistent explanatory
dimension. Specifically, whether ranking at inference time requires target-side
calibration of learned parameters appears to govern CPL viability. Models that rank using
pre-trained representation similarity (BLAZE) do not require calibration and can
operate in cold-start. Models that rank using a learned scalar (TRANP-CNN, COOBA)
do require calibration and fail in cold-start. This inference-time mechanism is
independent of training-time sophistication.

\subsection{Implications for CPL Research}

The low cross-model agreement (41--67\% across the three model pairs) is a
methodological contribution for the CPL research community. It establishes that
CPL benefit cannot be assumed to generalise across model architectures: a finding
from one CPL model does not reliably predict outcomes from a different architecture
on the same project pairs.

The counter-intuitive negative predictor for source quality in GNN-based adversarial
models (COOBA), where better within-project performance predicts worse transfer,
suggests that within-project performance and cross-project generalisability may
be competing objectives for this architecture class. High within-project performance
may reflect overfitting to source-specific AST patterns that do not generalise.
This motivates CPL-specific model design that explicitly prioritises cross-project
robustness over within-project peak performance, an open research direction not
addressed by existing proposals. Notably, this tension does not appear in
CNN-based or embedding-based rerankers, where source selection is essentially
irrelevant to transfer outcome.

Beyond architectural choices, this study yields several observations about how
future CPL evaluations should be designed. Results should be reported against a
\textbf{limited} within-project baseline in addition to, or instead of, the
ample-data ceiling: comparison against WP-large alone creates an inflated
impression of CPL benefit relative to the standard data-scarce practitioners can
actually achieve. The target label budget in the CPL condition should be
\textbf{matched} to the within-project baseline to avoid confounding additional
data access with cross-project knowledge. \textbf{Negative transfer rates} should
be reported alongside win rates and mean gains, since aggregate statistics conceal
the per-deployment risk profile that practitioners need for adoption decisions.
Finally, \textbf{zero-label cold-start viability} should be tested as a distinct
scenario: the cold-start result for embedding-based models is among the most
practically useful findings in this study, yet it is invisible under a standard
fine-tuning-only evaluation protocol.

\subsection{Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:threats}

\paragraph{Internal validity.}
All three models depend on FAISS retrieval at the first stage. FAISS recall at
$K = 300$ varies across the 13 projects in this study: from 31.3\%
(ansible) to 100\% (xarray), with a median of approximately 70\%. Three projects
fall below 50\% recall: ansible (31.3\%), wagtail (34.6\%), and meson (45.5\%).
For these projects, the reranking model never sees the ground-truth file in roughly
half to two-thirds of queries. Ground-truth files not retrieved within the top-300
candidates are automatically missed by the reranking model, imposing a shared
performance ceiling that suppresses absolute localisation metrics. The large-codebase
performance degradation observed in Table~\ref{tab:stratified} therefore conflates
model architectural limitation with retrieval ceiling. Disentangling the two would
require re-ranking-only evaluation over gold-standard candidate sets and is left to
future work. This ceiling does not threaten the within-model CPL comparison
(CPT vs.\ WPS), where both conditions face the same retrieval constraint.

\textbf{Training stochasticity and the fixed split.}
All train/test splits use a single fixed random seed (42), and every scenario was
retrained independently for each pair-run. The repeated WP-small runs provide a
direct estimate of run-to-run training variance: for a fixed target project,
identically configured WP-small runs differ by a mean min--max MRR spread of
0.07--0.10 across the three models (maximum 0.29). This variance is of the same
order as the mean CPL gains, so gain values for \emph{individual} pairs carry a
substantial noise floor. Two properties protect the aggregate conclusions. The
Wilcoxon analysis is paired at the run level, and re-testing CP-transfer against
each target's \emph{median} WP-small MRR across all of its runs preserves every
RQ1 conclusion (BLAZE $p < 0.001$, $r_{rb} = 0.96$; TRANP-CNN $p < 0.001$,
$r_{rb} = 0.69$; COOBA $p = 0.007$, $r_{rb} = 0.35$). Pair-level results, including
the illustrative cases in Section~\ref{sec:cases}, should nonetheless be read with
this noise floor in mind.

\paragraph{Construct validity.}
MRR and Top-$K$ measure file-level localisation. Finer-grained (function- or
line-level) localisation may show different transfer patterns if fine-grained
labels are available and the model architecture exploits them. The current
evaluation is aligned with the benchmarks used in prior CPL literature.

\textbf{Input-representation asymmetry.}
BLAZE reranks with modern frozen BGE embeddings (adapter-only fine-tuning), whereas
TRANP-CNN trains token embeddings from scratch and COOBA uses GloVe-300 features,
both faithful to their original designs. Representation quality therefore differs
across the rerankers and is bundled into each architecture under test rather than
controlled out. Three observations bound this threat: the candidate pool is
identical for all models, so retrieval quality is controlled; the two non-BGE
rerankers differ from each other ($d = 0.59$ vs.\ $0.27$), so
representation vintage alone does not determine CPL profile; and the cold-start
dichotomy is mechanism-driven, as detailed in Section~\ref{sec:architecture}.

\textbf{Re-implementation fidelity.}
TRANP-CNN and COOBA were re-implemented for this study (Section~\ref{sec:models}).
Loss functions, encoder structures, and adaptation mechanisms follow the original
publications, but the shared FAISS candidate stage and, for TRANP-CNN, the BGE
tokeniser vocabulary are departures from the original setups. The evaluated systems
are therefore faithful architectural adaptations rather than exact replicas, and
absolute scores are not directly comparable to those reported in the original
papers. The within-study comparisons are unaffected, since all models are evaluated
under the same adapted protocol.

\textbf{Pre-training data leakage.}
\texttt{BAAI/bge-code-v1} was pre-trained on a large multilingual code corpus that very
likely includes these popular open-source Python repositories~\citep{aleithan2024swebenchplus}. This exposes
the cold-start result for embedding-based models: the frozen backbone may already
encode project-specific representations from pre-training, partially inflating the
zero-annotation performance. We treat this as a construct threat. A proxy check
using project GitHub star counts as a proxy for pre-training prevalence shows no
significant Spearman correlation with cold-start MRR across the 13 projects
($|\rho| < 0.20$), suggesting pre-training familiarity is not the primary driver,
though a definitive test would require evaluating on private or newly created
repositories absent from pre-training corpora.

Bug-report-to-file mappings are taken from commit SHAs. Even with the
$>$10-file patch filter applied during curation, tangled commits that touch
unrelated files alongside the bug fix introduce residual label noise as a
construct threat.

\paragraph{External validity.}
The study covers 13 Python open-source projects. Generalisability to other
programming languages, proprietary codebases, or projects with different
bug-report vocabularies and styles is empirically unvalidated. The Python scope
is by design: cross-language CPL introduces additional syntactic and semantic
variation that is a distinct research question deferred to future work.
The mechanism-based explanations in Section~\ref{sec:architecture} are grounded
in inference-time properties (similarity-based vs.\ scalar-ranking) that are
language-agnostic in principle. However, COOBA's AST-based encoder is sensitive
to the structure of the AST vocabulary, including identifier namespaces, call-graph depth,
and type hierarchies, which differs between Python and Java. Whether
the COOBA CPL failure mode (near-zero cold-start, LoC-driven ceiling) replicates
in Java, where ASTs are more verbose and richly type-annotated, is an open
empirical question that the present study cannot answer.
Additionally, only open-source projects with publicly accessible commit histories
are included. Proprietary codebases may have different bug-report characteristics
or code organisation patterns. The embedding backbone (\texttt{BAAI/bge-code-v1})
is specific to the version used at evaluation time. A different or updated backbone
may produce different absolute numbers while leaving the relative architectural
conclusions intact.

\paragraph{Conclusion validity.}
The cross-model consistency finding is based on the shared pairs evaluated by all
three models. TRANP-CNN represents a third architectural data point with a medium effect ($d = 0.59$).
This places it intermediate between the large-effect embedding-based result (BLAZE, $d = 1.19$)
and the small-effect GNN-based adversarial result (COOBA, $d = 0.27$).
The Wilcoxon tests for each model are conducted independently per metric. The
Bonferroni correction applied within each test family controls the familywise error
rate across five metrics per model. The 63-pair set is a structured rather than
random sample of the 156 possible directed pairs. It over-represents DS$\times$DS
pairs (Group A, 30 pairs) relative to the full pair distribution. Aggregate statistics
over all 63 pairs should be interpreted in light of this design choice, though the
stratified analysis in RQ2 (Tables~\ref{tab:stratified} and~\ref{tab:crossdomain})
isolates within-domain from cross-domain effects.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Bug localisation models require labelled training data that newly started or
data-scarce projects often cannot provide. Cross-project learning has been proposed
as a solution. No existing work, however, has systematically compared CPL against
the practically relevant baseline of within-project limited training, nor tested
whether CPL findings hold across different model implementations.

We conducted a controlled empirical study across 63 source$\to$target project pairs,
13 open-source Python projects, and three state-of-the-art bug localisation models.
Four experimental scenarios were designed to isolate the contribution of cross-project
knowledge.

Our findings includes Cross-project transfer significantly outperforms within-project limited training for 2 of 3 architectural paradigms. CPL benefit is largest for small-codebase targets and within-domain pairs, but neither is a prerequisite for embedding-based models. No single project-level metadata feature reliably predicts pair-level CPL success for any model. CPL outcomes are architecture-dependent. The broader significance of these findings is that data-scarce projects no longer
need to wait years to accumulate sufficient labelled data before deploying bug
localisation. The deployment pathways revealed by this study are concrete.
Embedding-based tools can be applied immediately using any within-domain source
project with zero target annotations.

Future work will pursue four directions. First, extend the study to multi-language
CPL (Java, Python cross-language pairs, and Kotlin/Go) to test whether findings
generalise beyond Python~\citep{yang2024multi}. Second, develop model-specific source selection criteria
that go beyond project metadata, such as embedding-space proximity between source
and target corpora. Third, evaluate CPL at function and line level for architectures
that support fine-grained localisation. Fourth, investigate whether the
CPT$\approx$WPL ceiling for CNN-based rerankers can be matched by GNN-based models
through modified training objectives that decouple within-project and cross-project
performance.
